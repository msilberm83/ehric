Module 13, Lesson 2: Compare results with the pre-live baseline

Compare results with the pre-live baseline

The clinic bought the system to get a result. An honest comparison shows whether it did, and protects you from claims the data cannot support.

Benefits realization means checking whether the purchased outcome actually happened. The baseline is the measured value before go-live. You can only check benefits if someone captured the baseline before go-live, using the same definitions you use now. Ideally, the team agreed on each measure early in the project and captured its baseline before go-live. The AHRQ Health IT Evaluation Toolkit advises teams to decide early what is truly feasible to measure. (AHRQ is the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.) If there is no baseline, say so. Do not build one afterward from memory.

Follow these rules for every comparison:

  • use the same definition, population, and exclusions in both periods;
  • report changes in volume, staffing, payer mix, or method beside the result;
  • keep the result even when it is unfavorable;
  • do not claim that the system caused a change unless the evidence supports it;
  • do not count deferred features as delivered value.

Money needs extra care. Minutes saved per note are capacity released. That time is not revenue unless the clinic uses it to see more patients and collects for those visits. The clinic's financial owner decides how savings, costs avoided, and cash collected are reported. You present the measured facts and the limits of the data.

Rule: Compare like with like, report every method change, and let the financial owner decide how money is reported.

At Cedar Point, minutes per note fall by 2.5, but visits per provider stay flat. You report the saving as capacity released and send the revenue question to the finance director. At an allergy clinic, check-in time improves, but a new front-desk hire started the same month. You report the staffing change beside the result. In both cases, the report shows what the data can and cannot support.

Check yourself

  1. List three rules for an honest benefits comparison.
  2. Why is time saved not the same as new revenue?
  3. There is no pre-live baseline for a measure. What would you do?

Run the optimization backlog

Without one intake route, the loudest request wins. Safety problems reported by few people can wait behind popular small requests.

Optimization is planned improvement after go-live. It includes fixing friction, removing workarounds, and turning on features that were deferred. The backlog is the list of requests waiting for a decision or for work.

Take requests through one intake route

Each request records:

  • the requester, role, location, and workflow;
  • the problem in the user's own words;
  • how often it happens and the current workaround;
  • any safety, privacy, or billing effect.

Then sort it:

  • defect: the system does not do what was approved;
  • training gap: the system works, but users were not shown how;
  • enhancement: a change to the approved design;
  • policy question: a decision that belongs to a clinic authority;
  • new scope: work outside the contract.

Prioritize with the client's governance group

The governance group is the clinic's decision body for the system. You may propose a score, but the governance group decides the order. The 2025 SAFER Organizational Responsibilities guide calls for a decision structure for improving the EHR that includes user input. A simple score weighs patient-safety risk first. It then weighs how many people are affected, the revenue or compliance effect, and the effort. Safety risk moves an item up even when few people report it.

Send approved changes through change control

Every approved change gets an approver, a test plan, a regression check, a release note, and updated training and job aids. A request to remove a required clinical field goes to the clinical owner. A request to change a billing rule goes to the billing lead. You build and test what they approve. New scope goes to the account owner.

Rule: Every request enters through one route, and the owner decides before anyone builds.

At Cedar Point, a physician emails you to remove a required tobacco-use field. You log it through intake and send it to the clinical owner. At a dental-medical clinic, two reports say outside results land in an unowned inbox. The safety risk puts it above a popular print-button request. In both cases, the route and the risk set the order, not who asked loudest.

Check yourself

  1. Name the five request types.
  2. Why does safety risk move an item up even when only two people report it?
  3. A physician asks you directly to remove a required field. You have build rights. What would you do?

Keep training and the super-user network going

Skills fade, staff change, and workarounds grow. Ongoing training keeps the approved workflow in use.

The KLAS Arch Collaborative surveys clinicians about their EHR experience. Its public findings on training help with planning:

  • the quality of first training explains nearly half of the difference in how satisfied clinicians are with their EHR;
  • three to five hours of follow-up training a year is linked with higher satisfaction than two hours or less;
  • sessions of 15 to 60 minutes work well, and satisfaction does not rise with longer sessions;
  • training built around each person's own workflow is linked with less burnout.

Use support tickets to find topics. Six tickets a month on refill routing suggest a 20-minute session for the people who route refills. Move go-live training into new-hire onboarding with a named owner, usually the practice manager. The 2025 SAFER Organizational Responsibilities guide also asks for a plan that shows how users keep and update their EHR knowledge.

Reinforce the change. Celebrate early wins, and refresh training where workarounds appear.

Keep the super-user network going

A super user is a staff member with extra training who helps peers in their own area. Super users are usually named and trained before go-live. After go-live, your job is to keep the network going. Write down the role: hours protected for it, the topics covered, how super users report issues, and what they may not do. Super users coach and report. They do not approve configuration, clinical content, or access changes. Keep at least one per role group and location. Include a respected skeptic when possible. Replace super users who leave.

Rule: Base refresher training on ticket data, in short sessions built around each role's workflow.

At Cedar Point, providers ask for "more training," and tickets show refill routing and inbox filters. You draft short sessions on those two topics. At a pediatric clinic, a new front-desk hire starts in month two. The practice manager runs her through onboarding, and the front-desk super user coaches her on check-in. In both cases, the training targets the real gap for the role.

Check yourself

  1. What may a super user do, and what may they not do?
  2. Why use ticket data instead of a general survey to pick training topics?
  3. Who usually owns new-hire onboarding after go-live?

Manage vendor releases and upgrades

A vendor release can change screens, result routing, interfaces, reports, or AI features. An untested change can send a result to the wrong inbox.

The 2025 SAFER High Priority Practices guide asks organizations to test updates and local changes alone and with connected systems. It also asks them to watch systems for a short time after each change. Users should hear about changes that affect them before and after the change.

For each release:

  1. Read the release notes when they arrive. List affected workflows, interfaces, reports, and roles.
  2. Ask the vendor about anything the notes leave unclear.
  3. Run the risk-based regression set from Module 7 on the affected workflows in the test environment. A regression set is the group of earlier tests you rerun to check that a change did not break something that worked. If there is no separate test environment, use the compensating controls from Module 4 and test right after release.
  4. Update job aids and tell affected users what changes and when.
  5. Watch queues, interfaces, and support tickets closely for several days.
  6. Record the version, test evidence, and any defects.

The clinic's application owner approves the release date. Clinical content changes still need the clinical owner.

Rule: Test the release in the test environment first, then take the findings to the owner who decides.

At Cedar Point, release notes say abnormal results may route to a provider pool. You run the regression set on result routing and bring the findings to the medical director. At a dermatology clinic, a release changes the billing report layout. The billing lead reviews test output before release day. In both cases, the owner decided on tested evidence.

Check yourself

  1. List the six release steps in order.
  2. Why run the regression set before asking the clinical owner to approve a routing change?
  3. Who approves the release date?
