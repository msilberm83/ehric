Module 13 — Answers

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Confirm the steady-state handoff

  1. Any three of: new tickets go to the clinic's help desk or application owner; queue owners work queues without reminders; the clinic's named contact opens vendor cases; users know where to report problems.
  2. Quiet fixes keep the clinic dependent on you and hide the real load from its support team. When you leave, the work has nowhere to go.
  3. Which support tasks you still do under the contract, which you have stopped, and the date each one stopped.

Set the support model and service levels

  1. A written target for how fast support responds, updates, and restores service.
  2. Vendor targets bind the vendor through the contract. Internal targets are the clinic's own goals. Mixing them leads to promises the contract does not support.
  3. Bring the case evidence to the clinic's contract owner and the vendor's named escalation contact. Leave service credits and remedies to them.

Plan the 30, 60, and 90-day reviews

  1. 30 days: is the approved workflow in use? 60 days: are the measures moving? 90 days: did we get the purchased outcome?
  2. People can check the numbers before the meeting, so the review time goes to decisions.
  3. The sponsor, the operating owners, and the billing measure's owner.

Choose adoption measures the clinic can own

  1. First-pass acceptance counts claims that pass clearinghouse and payer front-end edits. The initial denial rate counts claims the payer reviewed and refused. They have different causes and fixes.
  2. People can log in without using the approved workflow or reaching the intended result.
  3. Check the definition, the population, time-out settings, and whether schedules are loaded in both systems. Then ask the measure owner to approve a matching definition.

Compare results with the pre-live baseline

  1. Any three of: same definition, population, and exclusions; report changes in volume, staffing, payer mix, or method; keep unfavorable results; do not claim causation without evidence; do not count deferred features.
  2. Time saved is capacity released. It becomes revenue only if the clinic sees more patients and collects for them.
  3. Say there is no baseline. Do not rebuild one from memory. Start measuring now with an agreed definition.

Run the optimization backlog

  1. Defect, training gap, enhancement, policy question, new scope.
  2. Patient-safety risk can cause harm even when it is rare, so the score weighs it first.
  3. Log it through the intake route and send it to the clinical owner. Do not build until the owner decides.

Keep training and the super-user network going

  1. They coach peers and report issues. They do not approve configuration, clinical content, or access changes.
  2. Ticket data shows where users actually struggle. A survey can delay a plan the data already supports.
  3. Usually the practice manager.

Manage vendor releases and upgrades

  1. Read notes and list impacts; ask the vendor about gaps; run the regression set in test; update job aids and tell users; watch the system for several days; record version, evidence, and defects.
  2. The owner needs to know how the change actually behaves in the clinic's build before deciding.
  3. The clinic's application owner. Clinical content changes also need the clinical owner.

Run or repeat the SAFER self-assessment

  1. ONC checklists that help an organization rate its own EHR safety practices. The High Priority Practices guide has 16 practices.
  2. The clinic's team is accountable for its safety practices. A rating made by the implementer is not the clinic's own assessment.
  3. In the 60 to 90-day window after go-live.

Record lessons and close the engagement

  1. Any four of: 90-day review held with benefits comparison; each measure owned with a review date; backlog owned by the governance group; open items listed with owners and routes; your access removed; sponsor signed.
  2. A specific practice can be used on the next project, and an owner makes sure someone acts on it.
  3. List the item in the closure record with its owner and decision route, then ask for sign-off.

Handle renewal and expansion talk honestly

  1. The vendor's account owner, with the client's decision-maker.
  2. It turns support into sales pressure and puts the clinic's safety and trust behind a purchase.

Worked example prompts

  1. The owners know their workflows and are accountable for their measures. Their explanation confirms the cause and keeps the decision with them.
  2. The implementer does not sell. Even an honest "no" is a buying decision that belongs to the account owner and the client's decision-maker.
  3. The cause was staff behavior at check-in, not the system. Training the front desk addresses the real cause.

Faded example

  1. Say that the cause is a missing backup owner, which an add-on would not fix. Send the purchase question to the vendor's account owner and the clinic's decision-maker.
  2. It is a gap in the approved design, so it is an enhancement (or a policy question if the clinic must decide who covers). The clinic's governance group decides its priority, with the clinical lead approving the owner.
  3. Change control: approver, test, regression check, release note, updated job aid. It closes when the backup owner works the queue on the nurse's day off and the paper list is no longer needed.
  4. Inbox task age against the baseline, whether the paper callback list is gone, and the owners and dates for any open items.

Knowledge check

  1. New tickets go to the clinic's help desk or application owner. Queue owners work queues without reminders. The clinic's named contact opens vendor cases, and users know where to report problems.
  2. Vendor service levels come from the contract and bind the vendor. Internal targets are goals the clinic sets for its own staff. If you mix them, you may promise response times the contract does not support, and remedies get confused.
  3. The first-pass acceptance rate counts claims that pass clearinghouse and payer front-end edits. The initial denial rate counts claims the payer reviewed and refused to pay. They have different causes and fixes, and the denominator (dollars or claims) must match the baseline.
  4. Use the same definition, population, and exclusions in both periods. Report method and volume changes beside the result. Keep unfavorable results, do not claim causation without evidence, and do not count deferred features as value.
  5. The clinic's governance group decides the order. The implementer runs the intake route, sorts requests, proposes a score with safety first, and builds and tests what owners approve.
  6. The implementer organizes the session, brings system evidence, and records the ratings and follow-up owners. The clinical lead and the clinic's team do the rating. The implementer does not fill in ratings, even as a draft.

Spaced review

  1. Stabilization should stay open. Exit criteria end it, not the calendar, and a low ticket count does not prove an unmonitored queue works. Propose an owner and a date to check the error queue.
  2. It must be in the one issue record, with the change, who approved it, and how the fix was checked. The training and permanent records must also be updated.
  3. A use-case statement. It names the owner, the data, the action, the result, and the control (who reviews or approves the draft, under what rule).
  4. Test the 835 remittance path: full payment, partial payment, a denial with reason codes routed to the denial queue, a zero-pay, and a takeback. Also match an EFT trace number to its 835.
  5. It names a task and a date, not an approved result. Rewrite it as a result, such as "All front-desk staff have shown competence on check-in, approved by the practice manager, by March 10."
  6. Responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed. The accountable person, such as the client data owner, approves the result.
