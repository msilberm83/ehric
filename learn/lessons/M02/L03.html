Module 2, Lesson 3: Run the internal handoff and track acceptance

Run the internal handoff and track acceptance

Teams that hear about work late cannot plan for it. The internal handoff brings your company's resource teams in before their work becomes urgent.

This book's method uses an email. That is a good default, because it creates a lasting record. A complex or high-risk item may need a follow-up meeting. The written request still belongs in the management system.

Each internal-handoff message answers seven questions:

  1. What is the project? Give the client, purchased outcome, relevant scope, and target window.
  2. Why is this team involved? Tie the request to a purchased item, requirement, or risk.
  3. What is needed? Name a deliverable or decision, not a vague request for thoughts.
  4. Who owns it? Address a named person, or ask the manager to assign one by a date.
  5. When is it needed, and why then? Connect the due date to the milestone that depends on it.
  6. Where is the record? Link to the management-system item where status and evidence belong.
  7. What happens if the date cannot be met? Name the escalation route.

Each team needs specific details:

  • Development: the approved requirement, acceptance statement, required date, affected workflow, and decision owner. Ask for feasibility and an estimate before anyone promises delivery.
  • Deployment: the environment, locations, install window, access method, client contact, prerequisites, checks, and fallback plan.
  • Technical and device support: device type, quantity, locations, model, network or workstation needs, who installs, and a representative test.
  • Data conversion: source systems, data categories, proposed scope, source owner, export dates, security needs, test-conversion window, and validation owner.
  • Integrations: both endpoints, business owner, technical contacts, contracts or enrollments, credentials, data flow, test environment, expected result, and failure owner. For a clearinghouse, list the account and each payer enrollment separately.
  • Quality control (QC): the roadmap, acceptance evidence, risk areas, module order, and review windows. Ask the QC lead to confirm the testing approach now. QC should not discover the scope for the first time during final testing.

Rule: A handoff is complete only when the receiving team accepts the work.

Example A. You send device support a complete request. Ten days pass with no reply. The handoff is still open, so you use the escalation route in the message.

Example B. Development replies that it can estimate the report but not by your date. You log the declined date and raise it with the dependent milestone's owner.

In both, the record shows what was accepted and what was not, so nobody assumes the work is covered.

Set the operating rules before kickoff

If the team agrees how it works only after problems start, each problem becomes an argument about process. Write a short operating plan before kickoff.

Communication. Define the core-team meeting, client status meeting, workstream meetings, and reporting frequency. Tie each meeting to decisions and work that cannot be handled by email. Include staff change communication: how clinic staff will hear why the clinic is changing and what comes next. A named client leader owns it.

Status. Use a simple status model with definitions:

  • Green: work is on plan, and no unresolved risk threatens a milestone.
  • Amber: a risk or issue could affect scope, date, safety, cost, or adoption, and it has an active response.
  • Red: a required outcome or milestone cannot be met without a decision or change.

Status should describe the project, not reward hope.

Escalation. Define what is escalated, to whom, and how fast. Safety, privacy, security, regulatory, financial, scope, and critical-path concerns should not wait for the next status meeting.

Change control. Change control is the process for requesting, analyzing, approving, scheduling, and communicating changes. Every material change shows its effect on scope, time, cost, resources, risk, training, testing, and earlier decisions. Work does not enter the build just because someone mentioned it in a meeting.

Record rules. Define where decisions, status, documents, test evidence, and restricted information belong. Do not use the project board in place of approved storage for protected data.

Rule: No change enters the build until its effects are analyzed and the right person approves it.

Example A. At a status meeting, the sponsor asks to add online bill pay to the patient portal. The order does not include it. You open a change request and analyze its effects before any build work.

Example B. The medical director asks to add an artificial intelligence (AI) tool that drafts replies to portal messages. You open a change request. It covers the intended use, the patient data the tool would receive, the vendor, privacy and security review, clinical approval, testing, training, monitoring, and cost. Planning continues on the purchased scope. Module 10 covers how AI uses are approved.

The requests differ, but both go through the same control before anything is built.

Worked example: an unsupported target date

Cedar Point Internal Medicine wants both locations live in January. The sponsor has told staff the date. Three dependencies are open. Billing setup is not one item: the clearinghouse account is active, but the claim and ERA enrollments with the payers are not signed. The signature pads have not been checked. The source vendor has not agreed to an export date.

  1. Agreed the rollout approach. The implementer asked the sponsor whether both locations go live together. Produced: a recorded decision, "both locations at once," made by the sponsor. Why: the choice sets how training, support, and conversion are planned.
  2. Planned backward from January. Produced: a date check. Correction, training, and testing fit only if the final conversion is done by mid-December. Why: this shows what the date really requires.
  3. Split billing into separate dependencies. Produced: roadmap entries for the clearinghouse connection, each payer's claim enrollment, and each payer's ERA enrollment. The billing manager owns each, with a need date. Why: "clearinghouse setup complete" was hiding the payer-side waits.
  4. Wrote each open dependency in full. Produced: entries for the signature-pad check and the export, each with an owner, need date, and effect of delay, and a decision date before kickoff. Why: a full entry can be chased and escalated; "pending" cannot.
  5. Found the critical path. Produced: the chain export → test conversion → correction → final conversion → client validation, marked as critical. Why: any slip in this chain moves go-live, so it gets the closest review.
  6. Labeled the date. Produced: January shown as a target pending validation, not as a commitment. Why: the sponsor told staff a date the plan does not yet support.
  7. Showed the sponsor the options. Produced: a short decision note: act earlier on the export, add resources, go live by phase, reduce scope, or move the date. Why: the sponsor decides; the implementer makes the trade-offs visible.

Why do you think the implementer agreed the rollout approach before checking the date?

Why was January labeled a target, even though the sponsor had announced it?

Why did the implementer give options to the sponsor instead of choosing one?

Field assignment

Build the preliminary roadmap for the Cedar Point Internal Medicine practice case:

  1. Enter the 13 module phases, grouped into the four stages, each ending at its gate.
  2. Record the rollout approach and who chose it.
  3. Create the workstreams, deliverables, milestones, and gates.
  4. Tie each purchased item to roadmap work.
  5. Add owners, accountable approvers, dependencies, evidence, and dates. List the clearinghouse account and each payer enrollment as separate dependencies.
  6. Identify at least one critical-path sequence.
  7. Label important dates by their basis: contractual, operational, external, resource-based, estimated, or target.
  8. Add the provider champion and super users to the responsibility table, marked "to be named by the clinic" where the case does not name them.
  9. Draft the internal handoff for each required resource team, and record acknowledgements, declined dates, and escalations.
  10. Add the communication (including staff change communication), status, escalation, record, and change-control rules.

Where the case does not give a fact, record it as open and name the role that would know. Use only the practice case. Never place real patient, client, or employer information in course work.
