Module 2 — Answers: Check yourself, examples, knowledge check, spaced review

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Turn scope into workstreams

  1. Any five of: governance and client decisions; deployment and environment readiness; workflow discovery and configuration; data conversion; interfaces and devices; privacy, security, and compliance review; training and adoption; quality control and testing; go-live readiness, activation, and follow-up.
  2. Whether it is a needed implementation task, an internal cost, or a scope change. A scope change goes through change control.

Break each workstream into owned work

  1. Any six of: outcome, deliverables, tasks, responsible person, accountable approver, dependencies, start and finish, acceptance evidence, and gate.
  2. It hides many tasks with different owners and approvers. Nobody can tell what is done, who is late, or what evidence shows it works.

Build the roadmap on the course's four stages

  1. Start the project; Design and train; Build the connected system; Go live and keep improving. Stage 3 holds Modules 8 (data conversion), 9 (integrations and configuration), and 10 (automation and AI).
  2. One map means the team, the client, and the course use the same phases and gates. Learners and clients do not have to translate between two maps or ask which one is right.
  3. Its gate. Work may start early, but the conversion must pass its gate before it goes to production.

Write milestones as results

  1. For example: "Test conversion accepted by the client data owner." Any result someone approves is correct.
  2. A task is work someone does. A milestone is an approved result or meaningful event that shows the project moved. A milestone tells the sponsor what is now true, not just what was done.

Plan backward from go-live

  1. All at once, by location, or by provider group. The sponsor chooses. You show how each choice changes training, support, and conversion.
  2. The go-live date is fixed as a target, so you need to know whether the required work fits before it. Planning backward shows the latest date each piece can finish, and exposes any gap.
  3. Show the sponsor the gap now, with options: act earlier, add resources, go live in phases, reduce scope, or move the date. Do not shorten correction time to make the date fit.

Label each date by its basis

  1. Contractual, operational, external dependency, resource-based, estimated, and target.
  2. The label decides how the date can move. A contractual date may need escalation or a formal change. A target date can move through normal planning.

Map dependencies and the critical path

  1. The deliverable, the provider, the receiver, the need date, and the effect of delay.
  2. "If this item slips, can go-live stay the same without cutting or squeezing required work?" If not, it is on or near the critical path.

Split billing setup into its separate enrollments

  1. Payer provider enrollment, clearinghouse account, claim (EDI) enrollment, ERA enrollment, and EFT enrollment.
  2. The clearinghouse connection is only one piece. Each payer must still approve claim enrollment, ERA must go to the new destination, and EFT must be confirmed. A test claim accepted by the clearinghouse does not prove any payer will accept or pay it.
  3. The client's billing or finance lead signs. You own the roadmap entries: one dependency per enrollment per payer, each with an owner, need date, and the milestone it blocks.

Assign one accountable person to each decision

  1. A staff member trained early who helps peers during training and at go-live. The clinic chooses its super users.
  2. Split it into separate questions, each with one approver, such as access design (privacy officer) and workflow feasibility (practice manager). Shared approval often means nobody decides.

Run the internal handoff and track acceptance

  1. What is the project? Why is this team involved? What is needed? Who owns it? When is it needed, and why then? Where is the record? What happens if the date cannot be met?
  2. QC must plan its testing approach and the evidence it will need. If QC sees the scope only during final testing, gaps are found too late to fix before go-live.

Set the operating rules before kickoff

  1. Green: on plan, with no unresolved risk threatening a milestone. Amber: a risk or issue could affect the project and has an active response. Red: a required outcome or milestone cannot be met without a decision or change.
  2. A named client leader. Staff hear the reason for the change best from their own leaders, and the clinic owns its culture. You help them repeat the message.
  3. Agreement in a meeting is not analysis or approval. The change must show its effect on scope, time, cost, risk, training, and testing. Then the right person approves it before it is scheduled and built.

Worked example prompts

  1. Rollout first. The rollout approach changes what has to fit before the date. Going live one location at a time spreads training, support, and conversion differently from a single go-live. The date check depends on it.
  2. January as a target. An announcement does not make a date credible. The plan does not yet show that the work fits, so labeling it a commitment would hide the risk from the sponsor and staff.
  3. Options, not a choice. Moving dates, adding resources, and cutting scope are business decisions with costs. The sponsor owns them. The implementer makes the trade-offs clear.

Faded example

A strong answer includes:

  • Step 3. March 2 is a target. The order calls it a target date, and the clinic manager's memory does not make it contractual. If the manager disagrees, the contracts owner checks the signed agreement.
  • Step 4. "The reference lab issues a test account to the integration lead by [date]; if late, lab interface testing and go-live move." The integration lead tracks it, and a named clinic or lab contact owns the request.
  • Step 5. Configuration → testing → training → readiness review → correction → go-live. The lab test account feeds the testing step and may join the critical path if it arrives late.
  • Step 6. Show the one-week gap now, with options: finish configuration earlier, add resources, go live with limited features, or move the date to March 9 or later. The sponsor decides.

Knowledge check

  1. A task is work someone performs. A milestone is a meaningful event or approved result, such as "future-state workflows approved." Milestones show progress the sponsor can rely on, not just effort spent.
  2. The roadmap shows that people, dependencies, review time, and correction time all fit before it. A date in a contract or a sales meeting is not credible on that basis alone. If the work does not fit, the conflict is raised now.
  3. Ask: "If this item slips, can go-live stay the same without cutting or squeezing required work?" If the answer is no, the item is on or near the critical path. Give it closer review.
  4. The clearinghouse connection only shows claims can leave the system. Each payer must still approve claim enrollment, ERA must go to the new destination, and EFT must be confirmed. Any one of these can block payment.
  5. It answers seven questions: what the project is, why this team is involved, what is needed, who owns it, when and why then, where the record is, and what happens if the date cannot be met. It also includes the team-specific details, such as endpoints for integrations.
  6. QC must plan its testing approach and required evidence early. If it first sees the scope during final testing, missing tests and unclear acceptance standards are found too late to fix.
  7. Its effect on scope, time, cost, resources, risk, training, testing, and earlier decisions. Then the right person approves or declines it before it is scheduled and built.

Spaced review

  1. It is a remembered commitment. List it as to be confirmed, with sales as owner and a due date. Plan on the documented scope until someone with authority confirms it. If confirmed but not covered, the commercial owner decides whether an approved change is needed. (Module 1)
  2. The first is a risk: it may or may not happen. The second is an issue: the conflict between the documents exists today. The test is "Has it already happened?" (Module 1)
  3. Add a success measure, such as "no-show rate per month," with a clinic owner such as the practice manager. The clinic sets any target, and the baseline is captured before go-live. (Module 1)
  4. The person doing the work is also approving it. Keep the trainer as responsible, and ask the clinic to name an accountable approver, such as the department manager. (Module 1)
