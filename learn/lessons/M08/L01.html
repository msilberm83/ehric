Module 8, Lesson 1: Name who owns and approves the data

Name who owns and approves the data

Data conversion touches clinical, billing, privacy, and legal rules. If no one with authority approves each choice, the vendor or the implementer ends up deciding things they do not own.

Data conversion moves selected information from the old system into the new one. It must keep the information's meaning, links, access rules, and required history. It is not a file copy. Every field and document carries context: who it belongs to, when it was made, how it was used, who may see it, and how long the clinic must keep it.

The source is the old system or file the data comes from. The destination is the new system.

Name the people who can answer and approve:

  • what information exists;
  • what is in scope;
  • which source is the trusted one;
  • what must stay accessible;
  • what may be changed during conversion;
  • how accuracy will be checked;
  • who may see the information;
  • when the source can become read-only or be retired;
  • which laws, contracts, professional rules, payer rules, or policies affect how long records are kept.

Each person has a different job:

  • You coordinate the conversion.
  • The client's data owner accepts the business meaning and completeness of the converted data.
  • Technical specialists extract, transform, load, and reconcile the data.
  • Privacy, security, records, clinical, legal, billing, and other leaders approve their own areas.

Do not let the vendor decide how long records are kept or when they are destroyed. Running the migration does not give the vendor that authority.

Rule: The vendor moves the data; the client's named owners decide what it means and what is kept.

  • Example A. The vendor suggests deleting old fax logs because "nobody reads them." You send the question to the records owner, who decides under the clinic's retention policy.
  • Example B. A billing specialist on the vendor team wants to drop balances under $5. You send it to the client's billing owner, who approves or rejects the rule.

In both cases, the person doing the technical work was not the person with authority.

Inventory every source

Information hides in more places than the main system. A source you miss becomes a gap staff discover after go-live.

List every source that may hold needed information:

  • the current application's database;
  • older legacy systems;
  • shared drives and document stores;
  • scanned charts;
  • paper records;
  • spreadsheets and local trackers;
  • email or messaging systems;
  • outside portals;
  • device or specialty systems;
  • billing, accounting, or payment systems;
  • archived exports and backup media;
  • interfaces that create or change records.

For each source, record:

  • owner and technical contact;
  • system and version;
  • data categories;
  • rough volume and date range;
  • format and export method;
  • data quality concerns;
  • identifiers and links to other records;
  • access limits;
  • retention and legal-hold needs;
  • planned destination or archive;
  • extraction date and what it depends on;
  • who will validate it.

A legal hold is an instruction to keep certain records because of a lawsuit or investigation.

Do not collect a broad export "just in case." Use the approved secure method, and limit access to the work needed.

Rule: Every source gets an owner, a format, and a planned destination before anyone extracts it.

  • Example A. Lakeshore stores scanned referral letters on a shared drive. They are not in the main system. You record the office manager as owner, scanned image files as the format, and each patient's document area as the destination.
  • Example B. A family medicine clinic keeps archived billing exports on backup media. You record the billing owner, the export format, and an approved archive as the destination. Extraction waits until all three are recorded.

Neither source was in the main system. Both needed an owner, a format, and a destination before extraction.

Sort the information into categories

Different kinds of information need different treatment. If you treat everything as simple fields, documents lose their links and history loses its meaning.

A simple starting framework sorts information into three groups: discrete data, manual entry, and scanned items. Those groups are still useful. A real conversion needs more detail.

Category What it is What to decide
Structured data Values in defined fields: names, IDs, dates, codes, balances, appointments, providers How each field maps. Matching names do not mean matching meaning.
Documents and scanned items Signed forms, letters, images, old charts How each is indexed, linked to the right record, secured, found, and kept. A file with no useful labels may be saved but impossible to find.
Manual or unstructured information Paper, narrative notes, local logs What staff need for ongoing work, who enters it, how it is checked, and whether the source stays available instead.
Derived or calculated values Balances, aging, scores, quality measures, calculated dates Whether to move the value, recalculate it, keep it as history, or leave it out. Each system may calculate it differently.
Relationships and identifiers Patient to visit, order to result, claim to payment, document to record How links are kept. Every value can be right and the conversion still fail if the links are wrong.
Audit and history Created dates, authors, changes, signatures, approvals, access events What must stay in the new system, in an approved archive, or both.
Excluded information Old temporary fields, duplicates, unsupported custom items, out-of-scope content Why it is left out, and how the client can still reach it or dispose of it under an approved rule.

For paper charts, agree a scanning plan. Decide which items get scanned before go-live, who labels and links them, and what stays on paper.

Rule: Classify information by how it must be treated, not by where it sits today.

  • Example A. An old system stores a "balance due" for each patient. The new system calculates balances from transactions. The billing owner decides to keep the old figure as history and recalculate the live balance.
  • Example B. Lakeshore has 8 years of scanned consent forms indexed only by patient name. The team plans to link each form by a validated patient ID, not by name.

Both items looked simple in the old system. Each needed its own treatment.

Set honest expectations and define scope

Clients often assume "everything will move." If you do not correct that early, every gap found later looks like a broken promise.

Tell the client early, in words like these:

"Not every item will move as the same kind of searchable data. Fields that look alike may not mean the same thing. We will convert the approved information through tested mappings. We will keep required history in the new system or in an approved archive. We will write down what changes and what stays outside the new system."

This is more accurate than promising that everything will move. It is also better than assuming all history should stay in the old system. The right choice depends on need, risk, retention, access, cost, and approval.

Define the scope

For each category, choose a treatment:

  • convert to a structured field;
  • convert as a document or attachment;
  • summarize, or have staff enter an approved subset by hand;
  • keep in a searchable, approved archive;
  • keep the source open read-only for a set time;
  • leave out under an approved disposal rule;
  • move it in a later phase.

Record date ranges, patient groups, status filters, locations, departments, and special groups. "Active patients" needs a definition. "Recent records" needs a date rule. The data owner approves the population.

Plan for work in progress. Upcoming appointments, open orders, unreviewed results, active claims, unpaid balances, open tasks, pending signatures, and open messages may need different treatment from closed history.

Rule: Every category gets an approved treatment, and every population gets a written definition.

  • Example A. Cedar Point's data owner says, "Move the active patients." You ask for a written rule. She approves "patients with a Lakeshore visit in the last 3 years."
  • Example B. A pediatric clinic has 10 years of old visit notes. The data owner approves a treatment: the last 2 years convert as documents. Older notes stay in a searchable, approved archive.

In both cases, a general wish became a written choice that the data owner approved.
