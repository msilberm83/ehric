Module 8, Lesson 2: Profile and clean the source

Profile and clean the source

You cannot map data you have not measured. Profiling shows problems before they reach the new system, where they are harder to find and fix.

Profiling means measuring the source data before mapping. Useful checks include:

  • record counts;
  • missing required values;
  • duplicates and likely duplicates;
  • invalid dates or codes;
  • mixed formats;
  • documents or transactions with no matching record;
  • odd characters, or values too long for the new field;
  • inactive and closed groups;
  • two records sharing one identifier;
  • broken links between records;
  • extreme or impossible values.

Cleaning needs rules and owners. Do not "fix" source data by guessing. For each issue, record:

  • the issue;
  • the affected group of records;
  • the proposed fix;
  • who approves it;
  • where the fix happens: in the source, during transformation, in the new system, or by hand;
  • the evidence and result.

Matching patients and merging duplicates can affect patient safety. Use the clinic's approved patient-identification process and qualified reviewers.

Rule: Every correction to source data needs a written rule and a named approver.

  • Example A. Profiling finds 300 phone numbers in three formats. The data owner approves one format, and the transformation converts them all.
  • Example B. Profiling finds 45 pairs of likely duplicate patients. The clinic's patient-identification reviewer checks each pair before any merge.

The first fix was low risk and could follow a rule. The second could put the wrong history in a chart, so a qualified person reviewed each case.

  1. List five things profiling measures.
  2. Why can't the implementer merge duplicate patients on her own?
  3. Profiling shows 600 birth dates of "01/01/1900." What do you record, and who decides the fix?

Map each field by meaning

Two fields with the same name can mean different things. A wrong mapping can put true data in a place where staff will read it the wrong way.

The mapping workbook records how each source item becomes a destination item. A transformation rule is the written instruction for changing a value on the way. For each item, record:

  • source system, table or file, and field;
  • source definition and sample format;
  • destination object and field;
  • destination definition and allowed format;
  • transformation rule;
  • what happens to blank or default values;
  • code or value translation;
  • relationship or matching rule;
  • access or sensitivity;
  • how it will be validated;
  • the accountable business approver;
  • the decision and version.

Do not force a mapping because two fields have similar names. "Status," "provider," "owner," "date," or "balance" may mean different things in each system. If no safe destination exists, send the decision to the data owner. Approved archive access is often safer than putting information in a misleading field.

Transformation examples

  • change a date's display format without changing the date itself;
  • translate old status values into approved new values;
  • split a full name only under an approved rule, with a process for exceptions;
  • keep leading zeros in identifiers;
  • convert time zones under a written rule;
  • link documents through validated record IDs;
  • mark where data came from, so users know it came from the old system.

That last mark is called provenance: a record of where information came from.

Every transformation should give the same result each time it runs. Give each version of the workbook a number.

Rule: Map by meaning, not by name; if no safe destination exists, the data owner decides.

  • Example A. The old field "Provider" holds the last provider who saw the patient. The new field "Primary care provider" means the patient's main doctor. They do not match, so the data owner decides.
  • Example B. The old "Status" field uses "I" for inactive patients. The new system uses "I" for insured. A direct copy would label inactive patients as insured. The team writes a translation rule.

In both cases the field names or codes matched. The meanings did not.

Protect the data

Conversion copies large amounts of patient data into new places. Each copy, error file, and staging area is a new way for that data to leak.

Staging is a temporary holding area where data waits between extraction and loading. Plan security for extraction, transfer, staging, validation, error handling, backups, and final disposal.

Write down:

  • the approved transfer method;
  • encryption, and who holds the keys;
  • access by role;
  • the staging location;
  • logging and monitoring;
  • how rejected records and error files are protected;
  • whether vendors or subcontractors create, receive, keep, or send protected or confidential information;
  • the contracts and approvals that apply;
  • how long temporary copies are kept and how they are safely destroyed;
  • the incident and escalation route.

HIPAA is the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Its rules protect health information. For HIPAA-covered work, the client and its business associates decide and document their own duties. A business associate is an outside company that handles protected health information for the clinic. Their duties include agreements, risk analysis, safeguards, access, audit, availability, and breach response.

A cloud provider that stores electronic protected health information may be a business associate. This is true even if the data is encrypted and the provider has no key. Use current guidance from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) and the clinic's privacy and legal review.

Rule: Every copy of converted data needs a protected place, a limited set of users, and a planned end.

  • Example A. Before a test load, the vendor backs up the Lakeshore staging area. You record where the backup is stored, which roles can open it, and when it will be destroyed.
  • Example B. At a dermatology clinic, an analyst copies patient photos to a laptop to check their labels. You move the work to the approved staging location and limit access by role. The laptop copy is destroyed under the plan.

Each copy was easy to forget. Each one needed a protected place, limited users, and an end date.

Run repeated test conversions

The first load almost never works. Repeated test runs let the team find and fix mapping problems before the real go-live load.

Use a non-production environment, or an approved test area kept apart from live work. A well-run conversion usually includes:

  1. a small technical sample;
  2. a representative test conversion;
  3. a corrected full-volume rehearsal;
  4. the final production conversion;
  5. checks after the load, and the move to live work.

Each run uses a controlled mapping version and produces:

  • source counts and checks;
  • extracted counts;
  • loaded, rejected, skipped, and transformed counts;
  • reasons for errors;
  • relationship checks;
  • data-owner validation;
  • defects and corrections;
  • run time and performance;
  • the evidence needed for the next gate.

Do not change mappings during a run without version control. The team must know which rules gave which result.

Rule: Every test run uses one numbered mapping version, so each result traces to its rules.

  • Example A. Cedar Point's second test run uses mapping version 3. Allergy reactions are missing after the load. Because the version is recorded, the team finds the rule in version 3 that dropped them.
  • Example B. At a family medicine clinic, two analysts used different copies of the workbook in one run. No one can tell which rule caused 200 rejected appointments. The team repeats the run under one numbered version.

A result is useful only when the team knows which rules produced it.

  2. Why must the mapping version stay fixed during a run?
  3. Halfway through a rehearsal, the vendor wants to fix a mapping row while the run is still going. What do you do?
