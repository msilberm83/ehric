Module 8 — Answers

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Name who owns and approves the data

  1. Any four of: what exists; what is in scope; which source is trusted; what must stay accessible; what may be changed; how accuracy is checked; who may see it; when the source can be retired; which rules affect retention.
  2. Destruction and retention are legal, records, and business decisions that belong to the client. Running the migration does not give the vendor that authority.
  3. Do not let it happen during the load. Send the duplicates to the clinic's approved patient-identification process, where a qualified reviewer decides each case.

Inventory every source

  1. Any six of: application database, legacy systems, shared drives, scanned charts, paper, spreadsheets, email, outside portals, device systems, billing or payment systems, archived exports, interfaces.
  2. It copies more protected data than the work needs, which adds privacy risk and storage the clinic must then protect and destroy.
  3. Owner and contact, what data it holds, volume and date range, format, quality concerns, identifiers, access limits, retention needs, planned destination, extraction date, and who validates it.

Sort the information into categories

  1. Structured data; documents and scanned items; manual or unstructured; derived or calculated; relationships and identifiers; audit and history; excluded.
  2. The links between records can be wrong. A result attached to the wrong order, or a document linked to the wrong patient, fails even if each value is correct.
  3. Ask whether the old balance was calculated the same way as the new system's balance. Raise it with the billing owner, who decides whether to move, recalculate, or keep it as history.

Set honest expectations and define scope

  1. Any five of: structured field; document or attachment; summary or manual entry of a subset; approved archive; temporary read-only source access; approved exclusion; later phase.
  2. Each person may mean something different, such as seen in the last year or three years. A written rule lets the data owner approve one population and lets testing check it.
  3. Explain that not everything moves as searchable data and that similar fields may differ in meaning. Ask the data owner to approve a treatment for each category and a definition for each population.
  4. Only discrete data converts cleanly. The coded allergy sits in its own field, so the new system can read it and fire an alert. The typed note moves only as text: staff can read it, but the system cannot act on it.

Profile and clean the source

  1. Any five of: counts, missing values, duplicates, invalid dates or codes, mixed formats, orphaned items, odd characters or truncation, inactive groups, shared identifiers, broken links, impossible values.
  2. Merging the wrong patients puts one person's history in another's chart, which is a safety risk. The clinic's approved patient-identification process and qualified reviewers decide.
  3. Record the issue, the 600 records, a proposed fix, the approver, where the fix happens, and the result. The data owner decides the fix, such as flagging the dates as unknown rather than guessing.

Map each field by meaning

  1. Any six of: source field, source definition and format, destination field, destination definition and format, transformation rule, blank or default treatment, code translation, matching rule, sensitivity, validation method, approver, decision and version.
  2. A misleading field can cause staff to act on true data with the wrong meaning. An archive keeps the information correct and reachable without that risk.
  3. Profile the field and ask the source owner what it means. Map it only to a destination with the same meaning, or send it to the data owner if none exists.

Protect the data

  1. Any five of: transfer method, encryption and keys, access by role, staging location, logging, error-file protection, vendor and subcontractor handling, contracts and approvals, temporary-copy disposal, incident route.
  2. Error files often hold the same patient details as the main data. An unprotected error file is a breach risk like any other copy.
  3. Check whether the transfer method is approved and whether the subcontractor is covered by the needed agreements. The client's privacy and security owners approve; email is likely not the approved method.

Run repeated test conversions

  1. Small technical sample; representative test conversion; corrected full-volume rehearsal; final production conversion; checks after the load and move to live work.
  2. If rules change mid-run, no one can tell which rules produced which result, so defects cannot be traced or fixed reliably.
  3. Do not allow the change in this run. Record the defect, fix it in a new mapping version, and test that version in the next run.

Reconcile and validate with the owners

  1. Any four of: control totals, field and relationship checks, business scenario checks, access and audit checks, client validation.
  2. Errors can cancel each other out. Two wrong balances can still add up to the right total.
  3. Schedule validation by the named business and clinical owners, using sample records and real tasks. A clean load report is not client acceptance.

Know why clinical lists need clinical validation

  1. Medications: status meanings differ, or drugs do not match a standard code. Allergies: free text does not code. Problems: resolved shows as active. Immunizations: names do not map to CVX, or dates are missing.
  2. "No known allergies" is a clinical statement that the patient was asked. A blank list means no one recorded it. Converting blank to "no known allergies" creates false safety information.
  3. Counts show that records moved. They cannot show whether an allergy is coded so alerts read it, or whether a status means what a clinician assumes.

Use three clinical safeguards and keep decisions with the clinical authority

  1. Flag, do not drop or guess; prepare charts before visits; reconcile at the first visit.
  2. Choosing a code is a clinical judgment. A wrong code can trigger the wrong alert or none. The clinical authority's rule decides how flagged items are handled.
  3. Send the report today with the list of unreviewed visits to the clinical authority. You track and report; you do not mark lists reviewed or change the target.

Plan the cutover and a real fallback

  1. A freeze stops changes in the source. A delta is the set of changes made after an extract. Fallback means returning to the old system if the new one cannot be used.
  2. It does not say what can be reversed, how long it takes, what would be lost or duplicated, who decides, or how users are told.
  3. A delta method for the changes, with an owner and a reconciliation step, or a freeze if the owners approve one.

Archive the history before you retire the source

  1. Any four of: required data converted or archived; users know where history is; access, search, export, audit, and retrieval work; contracts handled; retention approved; temporary copies handled; retirement approver recorded.
  2. It costs a lot of staff time and adds new typing errors. Most history only needs to be reachable, not re-entered.
  3. Do not retire the source. Raise the conflict with the retirement owner and the commercial owner, such as a short license extension or a read-only copy, until archive search is proven.

Worked example prompts

  • Why not just add referrals to the load? Open referrals were outside the purchased scope. Adding them silently changes scope without the commercial and clinical owners' approval, and hides cost and risk.
  • Why similar names and changed addresses? These are the records most likely to merge wrongly or match the wrong patient. Testing them shows whether matching rules are safe.
  • Why clinical approval too? The data owner accepts the data overall, but only a clinical authority can decide whether clinical lists are safe for care. The data owner relies on that review.

Faded example

  1. Do not guess the match. Propose holding the 1,150 files in an approved archive, flagged for review, or a manual matching process by qualified staff under the patient-identification process. The data owner approves, with the privacy owner.
  2. Source: document file, patient name, birth date, and any old system ID. Rule: link only through a validated patient ID or an approved match; mark provenance as legacy. Destination: the document area of the right patient's chart. Include approver and version.
  3. Count reconciliation of files; a sample check that documents open in the right chart; access tests showing restricted roles cannot see them; a business scenario where staff find a consent form; client validation by the document owner.
  4. Every required document is converted or in an approved, searchable archive; staff know where to find it; retention is approved; temporary copies are handled; and the retirement approver is recorded.

Knowledge check

  1. They are a useful first sort, but they miss important groups. Derived values, relationships, audit history, and excluded items each need their own treatment. Treating them as simple fields loses meaning, links, or required history.
  2. Fields with similar names can mean different things. A mapping by name can put correct data into a field where staff read it wrongly, such as a last-seen provider in a primary care field.
  3. The decision goes to the data owner. An approved archive or another approved treatment is often safer than forcing the data into a misleading field.
  4. Each value can be right while the links are wrong. A result tied to the wrong order or a document tied to the wrong patient is a failed conversion and a possible safety or privacy problem.
  5. Technical reconciliation shows that counts and totals match and differences are explained. Client validation is named owners confirming the data is accurate and usable for their work. Technical success is not client acceptance.
  6. What can actually be reversed, how long it takes, what information would be lost or duplicated, who decides, and how users are told.
  7. The count shows only that records moved. An allergy may arrive as free text that alert logic cannot read, or a blank list may convert as "no known allergies," so prescribers get no warning.
  8. The clinical authority approves it. The implementer produces flag reports, builds worklists, schedules reviews, tracks completion, and reports exceptions. The implementer does not judge clinical correctness, recode items, or mark lists reviewed.

Spaced review

  1. Regression tests on lists, screens, and reports that read medication status, such as the medication list, the drug-interaction check, and active-medication reports. The retest shows the fix works; regression tests show the fix did not break anything else.
  2. No. A pass belongs to one version in one environment. Version 3.3 changed rules, so you rerun the affected checks, plus the core set, on 3.3 before relying on it.
  3. It is a defect: the learner did nothing wrong, and the build linked documents to the wrong patient. Log it in the defect log and send it to QC with the steps to reproduce. Its severity is based on harm, and a wrong-patient document is serious.
  4. The client's privacy officer, with legal counsel where needed, decides. Your job is to ask the questions, record the decision and its owner, and build and test what was approved.
  5. As an external dependency, with the deliverable, provider, receiver, need date, and effect of delay. Give it an owner and a decision date, and show any go-live date that depends on it as a target until the export date is agreed.
