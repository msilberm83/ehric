Worked example: Lakeshore conversion scope

Cedar Point bought a conversion of demographics and future appointments for Lakeshore. Here is how the implementer handled what discovery found.

  1. Compared the purchase with discovery. Discovery showed that open referrals, unreviewed results, and active balances also matter for safe, continuous care and billing. They were not all in the purchased conversion. Produced: a gap list of three populations. Why: safe go-live depends on work in progress, not just on what was sold.
  2. Opened decision items instead of adding work quietly. The implementer logged each gap as a scope and decision item, with an owner. Produced: decision log entries for the clinical, billing, data, privacy, and commercial owners. Why: adding work silently would change the scope without approval.

Why do you think the implementer did not simply add open referrals to the next test load?

  1. Got a treatment decision for each population. The owners decided: open referrals move by controlled manual entry, unreviewed results stay in a read-only archive with a nurse review worklist, and active balances convert as a single opening balance per patient. Produced: an approved scope decision. Why: each population carries a different risk and a different owner.
  2. Built test cases from the hardest records. Test conversions included likely duplicate patients, similar names, changed addresses, appointments at both locations, missing provider values, and linked documents. Produced: a test-conversion plan with named sample records. Why: easy records pass; the hard ones show where mapping breaks.

Why did the test set include patients with similar names and changed addresses?

  1. Ran test conversion 1 and reconciled. The implementer explained every count difference and opened defects for the rest. Produced: a test-run report with reconciliation. Why: an unexplained difference may hide lost records.
  2. Got the validation rule approved. The data owner and the clinical safety representative approved the validation rule together. Produced: an approved, versioned validation rule. Why: the data owner accepts the data overall, but clinical lists need clinical approval.

Why did the clinical representative need to approve the rule, not just the data owner?

Your turn: faded example

If you have done this task on a real project, try this first, then compare with the worked example.

Lakeshore keeps scanned documents in a separate document system: consent forms, outside records, and letters. They must reach the new EHR or an approved archive.

  1. Inventory the source. Owner: Lakeshore practice manager. About 42,000 PDF files, 2017 to now. Indexed only by patient name and birth date.
  2. Classify it. Documents and scanned items. The link to each patient is a relationship that must be kept.
  3. Profile it. 1,150 files match two patients or no patient by name and birth date.
  4. Decide the treatment. What treatment do you propose for the 1,150 files, and who approves it? ____________
  5. Write the mapping row. How will each matched document link to the right patient in the new system? ____________
  6. Plan validation. Which checks prove documents are linked to the right patient and visible only to the right roles? ____________
  7. Set the retirement condition. What must be true before the old document system is turned off? ____________

Field assignment

Build a conversion plan for the Cedar Point Internal Medicine practice case (fictional; two locations, Cedar Point main and Lakeshore). Use a simulated source with synthetic data only. Do not use a real client, employer, or patient.

  1. Inventory sources, owners, formats, populations, risks, access, and retention needs.
  2. Classify structured data, documents, manual information, derived values, relationships, audit history, and exclusions.
  3. Define the approved treatment for each category.
  4. Profile a sample or simulated source.
  5. Build at least fifteen mapping rows with transformation and validation rules.
  6. Write the secure-transfer and staging plan.
  7. Plan at least two test conversions before production.
  8. Define count, total, field, relationship, scenario, access, and client validation.
  9. Draft a clinical validation rule for medications, allergies, problems, and immunizations for the clinical authority to approve, with the flag report, pre-visit worklist, and first-visit reconciliation tracking.
  10. Write production cutover, delta, fallback, communication, archive, and retirement steps.
  11. Role-play the required data, clinical, privacy and security, and release approvals.

Knowledge check

  1. Why are discrete, manual, and scanned categories useful but insufficient by themselves?
  2. What is the risk of mapping fields by name alone?
  3. What should happen when there is no safe destination field?
  4. Why must converted relationships be validated as well as values?
  5. What is the difference between technical reconciliation and client validation?
  6. What must be defined before the team can rely on a fallback or rollback plan?
  7. Why can a converted allergy list pass count reconciliation and still be unsafe?
  8. Who approves the clinical validation rule, and what does the implementer do and not do?
