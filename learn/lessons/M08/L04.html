Use three clinical safeguards and keep decisions with the clinical authority

The safeguards below catch clinical errors before and at each patient's first visit. They only work if the right people own each decision.

The three safeguards

1. Flag; do not drop or guess. Each test conversion produces a flag report. It lists uncoded or free-text items, unmapped codes, conflicting statuses, duplicates, and impossible dates. Flagged items arrive in the new system clearly marked as legacy or unverified. Do not quietly drop them. Do not let anyone assign a code by guessing.

2. Prepare charts before visits. For patients with visits in the first weeks after go-live, clinical staff review the converted lists before the visit. Build a worklist from the flag report. The clinical owner decides the order of review. High-risk patients often come first, such as those on blood thinners or insulin, or with severe allergies.

3. Reconcile at the first visit. At each patient's first visit in the new system, the clinician checks the converted lists with the patient and marks them reviewed. This matches normal medication reconciliation. For example, The Joint Commission's 2026 ambulatory National Patient Safety Goals ask clinics to find out what medicines a patient takes and compare them with new ones. Staff can also compare immunization history with the state's immunization information system (IIS), where one is available. An IIS is a confidential, population-wide database of vaccine doses.

During test conversions, clinicians also compare a sample of source charts with converted charts. Include high-risk patients and records with long lists.

Who decides

The clinical authority is the person named in the project's governance to make clinical decisions, such as the medical director or clinical lead. The clinical authority approves the clinical validation rule. The rule covers:

  • which items are flagged;
  • the sample size;
  • what must be reviewed before a visit;
  • how "reviewed" is recorded;
  • what stays in the archive.

The data owner accepts the converted data overall and relies on the clinical review for clinical lists. A pharmacist or nurse lead may advise.

You coordinate. You produce the flag reports, build the worklists, schedule reviews, track completion, and report exceptions to the clinical authority. You do not decide whether a converted allergy is clinically correct. You do not recode free-text items or mark lists reviewed. The vendor does not decide either, even when it runs the conversion.

Evidence to keep

  • the approved clinical validation rule, its version, and its approver;
  • flag counts by type and list, for each test and the final conversion;
  • the clinician sample results, with defects opened;
  • pre-visit review completion by location;
  • first-visit reconciliation rates after go-live;
  • open clinical exceptions, each with an owner and due date.

Rule: You produce and track the clinical checks; the clinical authority decides what they mean.

  • Example A. The flag report shows 120 free-text medications at Lakeshore. You build the pre-visit worklist and schedule the reviews. The clinical authority decides which patients come first.
  • Example B. At a pediatric clinic, 30 immunization records have no matching CVX code. The vendor offers to pick the closest code. You keep them on the flag report and send the decision to the clinical authority.

In both cases, you did the tracking work. The meaning of each clinical item stayed with the clinical authority.

Plan the cutover and a real fallback

Go-live weekend leaves little time to think. A written cutover plan decides in advance who does what, in what order, and what happens if the load fails.

Cutover is the planned switch from the old system to the new one. A freeze stops changes in the source so the final extract is complete. A delta is the set of changes made in the source after an extract. Fallback (or rollback) means returning to the old system if the new one cannot be used.

The cutover plan states:

  • the last date and time for work in the source;
  • the freeze, read-only, or delta approach;
  • the final extract and secure transfer;
  • the mapping and script version;
  • the load order;
  • who reconciles and validates;
  • time allowed for corrections;
  • go/no-go criteria and who decides;
  • the conditions for fallback;
  • communications;
  • source-system access after cutover;
  • how work done during the switch is handled;
  • how temporary files are destroyed;
  • support and monitoring.

Do not use "rollback" as a vague comfort. Define what can actually be reversed, how long it takes, what information would be lost or duplicated, who decides, and how users are told.

Handle deltas and work in progress

If users keep working in the source after an extract, decide how those changes reach the new system. Options include a final delta conversion, controlled manual entry, entry in both systems for a short approved period, or a cutover freeze. Each option has risk. Assign owners and a reconciliation step.

Rule: A fallback is real only when the plan says what can be reversed, how long it takes, and who decides.

  • Example A. The plan says, "If the load fails, roll back." You add the details: the old system stays read-write until 6 a.m. Monday, any visits booked in the new system before then are listed for re-entry, and the sponsor decides.
  • Example B. Lakeshore staff will book appointments in the old system all weekend after Friday's extract. You add a delta step with an owner and a count check on Sunday night.

Both plans seemed complete until someone asked, "What exactly happens?"

Archive the history before you retire the source

Once the old system is gone, missing history cannot be recovered. Retirement must wait until the clinic can still reach everything it must keep.

Before anyone retires the source, confirm:

  • required information is converted or available in an approved archive;
  • users know where old information lives;
  • access, search, export, audit, legal hold, and retrieval work as required;
  • contracts and licenses are handled;
  • retention and disposal are approved;
  • temporary copies are handled;
  • the person who approves retirement is recorded.

Do not re-type years of history without a proven need and an approved plan. Do not destroy or abandon it just because re-entry takes too long.

Rule: No source is retired until required history is reachable and the retirement owner has approved.

  • Example A. Lakeshore's old system is ready to retire. You test the archive. Staff can find old visit notes but not old payment history. Retirement waits until the billing owner can reach that history.
  • Example B. At a dermatology clinic, the archive works and users know where old records live. The vendor wants to switch off the source today. You first record the retirement owner's approval.

In both cases, retirement waited for two things: reachable history and a recorded approval.

Worked example: Lakeshore conversion scope

Cedar Point bought a conversion of demographics and future appointments for Lakeshore. Here is how the implementer handled what discovery found.

  1. Compared the purchase with discovery. Discovery showed that open referrals, unreviewed results, and active balances also matter for safe, continuous care and billing. They were not all in the purchased conversion. Produced: a gap list of three populations. Why: safe go-live depends on work in progress, not just on what was sold.
  2. Opened decision items instead of adding work quietly. The implementer logged each gap as a scope and decision item, with an owner. Produced: decision log entries for the clinical, billing, data, privacy, and commercial owners. Why: adding work silently would change the scope without approval.

Why do you think the implementer did not simply add open referrals to the next test load?

  1. Got a treatment decision for each population. The owners decided: open referrals move by controlled manual entry, unreviewed results stay in a read-only archive with a nurse review worklist, and active balances convert as a single opening balance per patient. Produced: an approved scope decision. Why: each population carries a different risk and a different owner.
  2. Built test cases from the hardest records. Test conversions included likely duplicate patients, similar names, changed addresses, appointments at both locations, missing provider values, and linked documents. Produced: a test-conversion plan with named sample records. Why: easy records pass; the hard ones show where mapping breaks.

Why did the test set include patients with similar names and changed addresses?

  1. Ran test conversion 1 and reconciled. The implementer explained every count difference and opened defects for the rest. Produced: a test-run report with reconciliation. Why: an unexplained difference may hide lost records.
  2. Got the validation rule approved. The data owner and the clinical safety representative approved the validation rule together. Produced: an approved, versioned validation rule. Why: the data owner accepts the data overall, but clinical lists need clinical approval.

Why did the clinical representative need to approve the rule, not just the data owner?
