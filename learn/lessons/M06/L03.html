Worked example: behavioral health note access

Cedar Point adds a licensed clinical social worker at Lakeshore. The approved design gives her a separate psychotherapy note type. Only she can open it.

  1. Confirmed the decision owners. You checked the decision record. The behavioral health lead and the privacy officer had approved the note type and access. Produced: an authority list for the workflow. Why: you build and train only what the owners approved.
  2. Wrote access scenarios. You wrote a scenario for each role and output: provider, medical assistant, front desk, release of information, and the portal. Produced: an approved access test set. Why: protection must be proven for every path a note can take.
  3. Ran the medical assistant scenario. In class, a medical assistant prepared the next day's charts using synthetic records. She could see the title and date of the psychotherapy note, though not its text. Produced: an observation in the class notes. Why: real tasks expose gaps that a feature list misses.
  4. Stopped and sorted the finding. The design said only the author can open the note. It did not say whether others may see that it exists. You logged a decision item for the privacy officer and a possible defect for the builder. The class went on with other scenarios. Produced: two register entries. Why: a gap in the design needs the owner's decision before anyone changes the build.
  5. Got the decision. The privacy officer and behavioral health lead decided that no other role may see the title. Produced: a recorded decision. Why: access to protected notes is their call, not yours.
  6. Requested the fix and the retest. The builder changed the view. QC retested the medical assistant, provider, front-desk, and release-of-information roles. QC also checked that the note stays out of the portal and the release packet. Produced: a QC request and test evidence. Why: one fix can break another path, so every role and output is retested.
  7. Rechecked the learner. The medical assistant repeated the scenario on the fixed release. Produced: an updated competence record showing the recheck. Why: her earlier result was on a build that no longer exists.

Prompt 1: Why did you log both a decision item and a possible defect, instead of only a defect?

Prompt 2: Why did QC retest roles that had passed before?

Prompt 3: Why did the class continue with other scenarios after the finding?

Your turn: faded example

If you have done this task on a real project, try this first, then compare with the worked example.

Clinical staff at a family clinic practice a synthetic after-hours triage request. The approved protocol sends urgent items to an on-call pool. In the training build, every item goes to the daytime queue.

  1. Stopped the scenario. The instructor paused the exercise when the urgent item landed in the daytime queue. Produced: a note of the scenario, release, and result. Why: continuing would teach the wrong routing.
  2. Classified the finding. The approved protocol already states the rule, so the build does not match an approved design. Produced: a high-priority defect entry. Why: no new decision is needed; the build is wrong.
  3. Your step: What does the class use for the rest of the exercise, and why?
  4. Your step: Who confirms the expected rule, and who fixes the build?
  5. Your step: What must QC retest before the scenario is used again?
  6. Your step: What happens to the learners who practiced on the faulty build?
