Module 6 — Answers

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Name the domain authority. (1) Help owners reach decisions, turn approved decisions into requirements, and check that the build and training match. (2) Questions come up fast in class. If no owner is named, people turn to you, and you end up deciding things outside your role. (3) Send the question to the clinical owner, such as the medical director. Once she sets the threshold, build and teach it.

Recheck readiness before training. (1) Any four: approved workflows, approved scenario set, QC passed, correct permissions, ready or simulated templates and devices, plan for known defects, synthetic data, prerequisites done, support routes ready. (2) Staff learn steps that may change, and then must unlearn them before go-live. (3) Use that block for a decision session with the owner, and train the approved parts.

Train triage as a routing process. (1) Clinical leaders. (2) A late acknowledgment shows whether overdue urgent work becomes visible, and who acts on it. (3) Do not share other clinics' practice as advice. Raise the gap with the protocol owner and show how the system routes the call once she decides.

Train visit documentation. (1) Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan. (2) Coding, coverage, payer rules, and billing controls are separate steps with their own owners. Skipping them can produce wrong claims. (3) Teach the approved review of carried-forward information. If the provider wants a different rule, log it for the clinical owner.

Include converted medication and allergy lists. (1) Clinical leaders. (2) Interpreting a clinical entry is a clinical judgment. The provider confirms it with the patient through the approved step.

Close the loop on orders and results. (1) Order, transmission, receipt, review, communication, follow-up, closure. (2) The result may never reach a clinician, so an abnormal finding can go unseen. (3) Ask the owner of that watch item to set an interval before the module passes, and record it.

Ask disciplined questions about specialty work. (1) Any three of the seven listed questions. (2) It exposes the risks the scenarios must test, so training covers the failures that could harm a patient.

Ask who decides about behavioral health notes. (1) Any two: medicines, session times, test results, summaries of diagnosis, treatment plan, symptoms, or progress. (2) Protection depends on what the note holds and on keeping it apart, not on its name. (3) Explain that this is a legal question for the privacy officer and counsel. Send it to them and hold the affected build.

Follow the information downstream. (1) Any three of the six listed questions. (2) Missed handoffs fail silently. Naming who notices them is what makes the failure visible.

Use progressive practice. (1) Orientation, demonstration, guided practice, independent practice, exception practice, teach-back, competence decision. (2) Clinical competence is a professional judgment the client assigns to a named evaluator. (3) Give your observations to the named evaluator and have her make and record each decision.

Route what training reveals and collect approvals. (1) To the privacy officer. (2) The fix changes no clinical content. It fits the approved low-risk process, so you record it and send it to QC. (3) For example: "Clinical lead approves the cardiology template text by June 3; builder loads it by June 5; QC tests it by June 7."

Worked example prompts

  1. The design was silent on whether others may see the note exists, so the owner had to decide. The build might also be wrong, so a possible defect was logged too.
  2. A change to one view can expose the note through another path. Retesting every role and output proves the fix did not break anything else.
  3. One finding should not waste the session. Other scenarios were unaffected, and the finding was safely logged with its owner.

Faded example

  1. The class uses the approved downtime route for the exercise. This keeps learners practicing the approved process while the build is wrong.
  2. Clinical leadership confirms the expected rule. The build team corrects the routing.
  3. QC retests both the normal (daytime) case and the after-hours case, so the fix does not break the normal path.
  4. Affected learners repeat the scenario on the fixed release, and their competence records show the recheck.

Knowledge check

  1. You help the domain owners reach decisions, turn approved decisions into system requirements, and check that build and training match them. You also own scenarios, routing of findings, QC requests, and records. You do not make clinical, legal, billing, or privacy decisions.
  2. Triage decides how urgent a patient's need is, which is a clinical judgment with safety risk. Only qualified clinical leaders can set and approve that protocol. The implementer teaches how the system carries it out.
  3. Notes, coding, coverage, payer rules, and billing controls are separate steps with separate owners. Teaching that a note is automatically billable invites staff to skip coding review and send wrong claims.
  4. The behavioral health clinical lead decides, with the privacy officer. Once decided, you build the note type and role access, write scenarios that prove the controls, train each role, and test that the note stays out of places it should not reach.
  5. Specialized work starts downstream tasks such as orders, coding, and follow-up. Tracing them shows who notices a missed handoff and what QC must check, so training covers the whole process, not isolated clicks.
  6. The approved evaluator the client names, such as a medical director, preceptor, or supervisor. The implementer records observations and results but does not certify clinical competence.

Spaced review

  1. Schedule a short refresher on the refill workflow for the roles it affects, using release 3.3, and recheck that scenario. Competence must match the release that goes live (Module 5).
  2. Explain that super users should be people peers trust and go to for help, with protected time and backfill. The clinic makes the final choice (Module 5).
  3. Do not agree or refuse it on the spot. Send it to the change process, and record it as an open item with an owner and date (Module 3).
  4. Name the deliverable, provider, receiver, need date, and effect of delay. For example: "The lab issues test credentials to the interface team by May 2; if late, interface testing and training move" (Module 2).
  5. Mark it as a remembered commitment, not a documented one. Log it with an owner and due date to confirm with sales and the client (Module 1).
