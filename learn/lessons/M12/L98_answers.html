Module 12 — Answers

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Confirm the decision still holds before activation

  1. Any four of: approved production version; final conversion and reconciliation state; critical interfaces, devices, access, and queues; staffing and contacts; downtime readiness; issue record and channels; must-pass criteria and pause authority; announcements; accepted limitations and controls.
  2. The approval covered the facts on Friday. A change in a must-pass condition since then means the clinic would go live on a risk nobody approved.
  3. Hold activation and ask the person who approved go-live for a new decision. A promised fix is not a passed test.

Open one command function and one issue record

  1. Any five of the listed fields, such as issue ID and time, reporter and workflow, severity, owner, decision and authority, status, correction version, and closure.
  2. Nobody else can see what changed, who approved it, or how the fix was checked. The change may have side effects that appear later.
  3. The vendor ticket sits outside the shared picture. The command team needs every change, decision, and test in one place.

Triage each report by impact

  1. Is there an immediate safety, confidentiality, security, legal, financial, or data-integrity risk? It comes first because harm to patients, data, or money outranks reach and convenience.
  2. Check workflow, screen setup, configuration, access, data, and usability. Trained users repeating one error point to the build or workflow.
  3. Any three of: unfamiliarity, local setup problem, defect, data problem, integration failure, policy question, incident.

Control changes made during go-live

  1. Tell affected users; validate after release; update training and permanent records.
  2. The approver owns the risk of the change. Without one, a single person changes the live system on their own judgment.
  3. No. The job aid and permanent records are part of the change. Users following the old job aid will make errors.

Reconcile each operating period

  1. Any four of: expected versus completed transactions, failed or pending interfaces, duplicate or unmatched records, conversion exceptions, overdue clinical work, failed forms, automation errors, access issues, support volume, critical defects, downtime back-entry.
  2. Some failures produce nothing for a user to see. A result that never arrives cannot generate a ticket.
  3. Log an issue for the gap, assign it to the interface owner today, and report it in the status.

Run the end-of-day huddle

  1. What affected operations today? What was contained or corrected? What remains open, and what is the overnight risk? What must be ready next? Does the go-live decision remain valid?
  2. Conditions change during go-live. A new risk may cross a pause or downtime trigger.
  3. Operating state, main volume and reconciliation result, critical and high issues with controls, decisions needed, next actions, and next update time.

Use downtime or pause when a trigger occurs

  1. Validate recovery; reconcile deferred work; get authority to resume.
  2. The vendor confirms that the system is reachable, not that clinic work flows correctly. The procedure also names who authorizes resuming.
  3. Staff record it during downtime. After recovery, it is entered and reconciled.

End stabilization on evidence, not the calendar

  1. Any four of the listed criteria, such as no unresolved critical defects, workflow volumes reconcile, workarounds removed or accepted, and owners can run monitoring.
  2. Every exit criterion may already be met with evidence before the planned date.
  3. Check the evidence for every exit criterion, especially processes that nobody is monitoring.

Follow up on a schedule

  1. Adoption, repeat questions, workarounds, backlog, correction patterns, role coverage, open defects, data quality, automation, and support ownership.
  2. The clinic paid for that result. Comfort and ticket counts do not show whether it happened.
  3. When the launch is complex or high-risk, or the contract requires it.

Transfer to operations and support

  1. Known issues and workarounds, the configuration record, open defects, and who to call for each integration.
  2. A signature shows the receiver got the document. A demonstration shows they can do the work.
  3. Do not record the transfer as complete. Coach or retrain, then repeat the demonstration, and record the result.

Close the implementation

  1. Any five of the nine listed items.
  2. No one owns it there. It looks finished when it is not.
  3. Optimization is new work with its own owners and measures. Mixing it in makes the implementation look unfinished, or hides real open scope.

Worked example prompts

  1. A quiet support line can hide failures that nobody monitors. Checking every criterion is the only way to know which ones are met.
  2. Workflow decisions and accepting a workaround belong to the clinic's operations owner. The specialist reports and coordinates but does not accept operational risk.
  3. QC proves the build meets the requirement. Users repeating the workflow proves people can do the real work with it.

Faded example

  1. Report that stabilization should stay open. Two of three criteria are not met. Recommend owners and dates rather than a new end date.
  2. The interface owner owns the results errors. The clinic's operations or front-desk owner owns the check-out workaround and the workflow decision.
  3. Send it through change control: approver, test, regression check, user notice, updated job aid. It closes when QC passes and front-desk staff complete check-out without the paper slip.
  4. Results errors are cleared within one business day for the agreed period, and the paper slip is removed. Or the right authority formally accepts the limitation and names its owner.

Knowledge check

  1. Go-live moves the clinic from planned test scenarios to real patients, volume, and consequences. Issues, changes, reconciliation, and support work continue until the clinic can run the system on its own. The project closes only after stabilization, transfer, and closure.
  2. Issue ID and time, reporter and affected workflow, description and evidence, impact and severity, workaround, owner, related vendor cases, decision and authority, status, correction version, test and confirmation, affected users or records, and closure. It is the one shared picture for the command function.
  3. Urgent fixes in the live system can change access, routing, billing, data, or reports in ways nobody notices at once. The emergency path keeps a named approver, testing, a fallback, validation, and updated records, even when it moves fast.
  4. Approved exit criteria, reviewed by workflow and risk, end stabilization. The planned date alone does not. Each criterion must be met with evidence or formally accepted by the right authority with a named owner.
  5. The receiver demonstrates the monitoring and support tasks while you watch, and you record the result. A document shows only that information was sent. The knowledge pack gives the support team what it needs to act.
  6. They may call themselves a holder of the Electronic Health Record Implementation Certificate. They may not say "certified," add letters after their name, or imply licensure. They may not claim clinical, legal, billing, privacy, security, or product certification.

Spaced review

  1. Report by readiness area, not by one percent. Lead with the failed restore test as a must-pass blocker with its owner. Percent complete can hide a single critical failure.
  2. Add an aging rule, an escalation route, and a backup owner, approved by the accountable domain owner. A queue with no aging rule can hide work forever, and an alert nobody owns is noise.
  3. A 999 shows only that the file met X12 syntax rules. It does not show that each claim passed front-end edits (277CA) or was paid (835). Test rejections, denials, and remittance before calling claims ready.
  4. Explain every count difference. Then the named data owner validates the records and records acceptance or open defects. A percentage alone is not acceptance.
  5. It is a remembered commitment, because it is not in the signed order, statement of work, or an approved change. Record it in the commitments table with an owner and due date to confirm or resolve it.
  6. The decision and action record should have gone out within one business day, with all dates placed on the shared calendar. Late records let people act on different memories of the meeting.
