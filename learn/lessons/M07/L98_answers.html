Module 7 — Answers: Check yourself, examples, knowledge check, spaced review

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Decide what "it works" must mean

  1. Any three of: the screen loads; the normal path completes; the requirement is met; the right person gets the next work; unauthorized actions are blocked; the record is complete and auditable; exceptions are caught and recovered; the workflow stays safe when an interface is down; trained users do it consistently.
  2. The requirement may demand more than saving, such as routing, access control, or a complete audit record. A screen can work technically and still fail the clinic's approved need.
  3. Check the approved requirement first. Then confirm the referral reaches the right person or queue, the right roles can and cannot act on it, failure states show, and the record is complete.

Finish advanced workflows and train the failure path

  1. Any four of: what leaves the system; what should come back; normal response time; where success, pending, rejection, or failure shows; who owns each side; what to do when it is slow or down; what must never be repeated or bypassed.
  2. It names a field of care, not a workflow. You still need to learn the actual templates, devices, results, rules, and handoffs from the person who owns the work.
  3. Recognize the pending state as a possible failure, avoid resending, and follow the approved route to the owner of that queue, such as the billing lead.

Control the build that QC receives

  1. Any four of: release purpose and contents, source and target environment, what is included (configuration, manual steps, vendor settings, data, interfaces), builder checks done, promotion method, known limits, correction path, release approver.
  2. Production may differ from test, for example through manual settings or a failed promotion. A pass is proof only for the version and environment tested.
  3. Record the new version, decide which earlier results are still valid, and rerun the tests the change could affect.

Build the QC plan from requirements

  1. Entry: requirement and approver named; stable build; environment and access ready; test data available; known limits written; expected results defined. Exit: planned tests done; evidence recorded; Critical/High handled; fixes retested; regression recorded; limits approved; training materials updated; version approved.
  2. Exploration may find defects, but it does not link tests to requirements and risks. Without those links you cannot show that every important area was checked.
  3. "Training materials and job aids updated if behavior changed." You own the materials, so you update the job aid, then submit the version for approval.

Design reproducible test cases

  1. Any six of: test ID and requirement, purpose or risk, environment and build, role and access, data or preconditions, steps, expected result, actual result, pass or fail, evidence, tester and date.
  2. The busiest hour is when slow responses would delay patients and staff. A test after hours can pass while the front desk still stalls at 8 a.m.
  3. A permission and segregation test.

Rate defects by impact

  1. Severity is how much harm a defect can cause. Priority is the order the team works on defects. They can differ.
  2. Severity is about harm, not effort. A one-line fix can still prevent a safety or privacy failure, so it may be High or Critical.
  3. A retest of the original failure, and regression tests on the behavior around it.

Retest the fix, then run regression tests

  1. A retest asks whether the original failure is fixed. A regression test asks whether the fix or another change broke something else.
  2. The partner's side changed, so messages in both directions may behave differently. An interface change on either side is a trigger.
  3. Any four of: defect fix, configuration change, vendor upgrade or patch, interface change, data change, environment change, promotion.

Choose and record a risk-based regression set

  1. Start from the change record; trace what it touches; add neighbors; keep a core set; rank by risk; record what you left out.
  2. It stops the same failure from quietly returning in a later build.
  3. The two tests, the real risk-based reason (not just "time"), and the release owner's acceptance of that risk.

Protect QC independence

  1. Any four of: peer review, approved test scripts, recorded evidence, client user acceptance, specialist approval for controlled areas, release decision by someone other than the builder.
  2. Independence means a reviewer who can challenge the result and report freely. A peer, a client user, or a separate approver can provide that in a small team.
  3. Keep her testing, but add peer review and approved scripts, and name someone else to make the release decision.

Run QC after every module

  1. Build, builder check, QC, correct, retest and regression, approve, train or release.
  2. Defects found in class waste learners' time, and learners may practice on a build that will change. Training should confirm a tested build, not test it for the first time.
  3. Back to retest and regression, then approval, before it is trained or released again.

Keep user acceptance separate from QC

  1. QC asks, "Does it meet the requirement?" UAT asks, "Can our people do the work with it?"
  2. It does not show which scenarios, users, environment, or results were involved, so no one can check what was accepted.
  3. Record the finding and route the access question to the privacy officer. The nurse cannot waive a privacy control she does not own.

Record the release decision

  1. One identified version for one named purpose: training, UAT, rehearsal, or production.
  2. A version may be fit for training but not for production. The purpose tells everyone exactly what the approval covers.

Worked example prompts

  • Why the billing owner? The 30-minute rule is business policy about who watches billing work. The client owns that policy. The implementer's job was to find the gap and route it to the person with authority.
  • Why test duplicate prevention? A user facing a stuck "pending" check is likely to resend it. The new alert changes how staff react, so QC must prove that a second request does not create a duplicate.
  • Why update the job aid first? Exit criteria require training materials to match changed behavior. Releasing with an old job aid would teach staff the wrong steps.

Faded example

  1. Retest: in build 5.5, sign in as the front-desk role, slow or delay the fax response, click "Send" twice, and confirm only one fax goes out. Record the result beside the failed result from 5.4.
  2. Regression set (any three, with reasons): a normal single referral fax (same service); a rejected or failed fax (same send logic); other outgoing faxes such as records release (shared fax service); referral fax from the Lakeshore location (same feature at another site); the core set (standard after every build).
  3. Change and build ID; each test and why it was chosen; environment, build, tester, and date; new results beside earlier passing results; any new defects linked to the fix; tests not rerun, the reason, and who accepted that risk.
  4. The release owner decides. The purpose is release of build 5.5 for its next stage, such as training. Hand over the defect record, retest and regression records, exit-criteria status, open Critical and High defects, and any accepted risks.

Knowledge check

  1. A build can run without errors and still miss the approved requirement. It may route work to the wrong person, allow an action a role should not take, or break a privacy, billing, or safety control. QC tests against the requirement, not just against error-free behavior.
  2. It records everything another person needs to repeat it: requirement, purpose, environment and build version, role and access, data or preconditions, steps, and expected result. It also records the actual result, evidence, tester, and date, so the outcome can be checked.
  3. Severity is the harm a defect can cause. Priority is the order in which the team works on defects. An easy fix can be Critical, and a hard fix can be Low.
  4. Someone saying "fixed" is not evidence. The retest proves the original failure no longer happens in the corrected version. Only then can regression testing check what else the fix touched.
  5. A retest checks that the original failure is fixed. A regression test checks that the fix or another change did not break something that passed before. Triggers include defect fixes, configuration changes, vendor patches, interface or partner changes, data changes, environment changes, and promotions.
  6. Start from the change record and trace what it touches. Add tests that share parts with the change, run the core set, and rank by likely effect and harm. Record what you left out, and have the release owner accept that risk.
  7. Add peer review, approved scripts, recorded evidence, client user acceptance, and specialist approval where needed. Give the release decision to someone other than the builder.
  8. QC asks whether the build meets the approved requirements and handles risks. User acceptance asks whether authorized client users can do the approved work with it. Neither replaces the other.
  9. Different environments and versions can behave differently, for example because of manual settings or failed promotions. A release purpose also limits what the pass supports. A pass carried forward without a check is not evidence.

Spaced review

  1. Do not change it. A clinical template change goes to the clinical owner and may need billing, safety, build, training, and QC review. Record it in the register with an owner and date, and continue the session.
  2. Not yet. Attendance is not competence. She must complete normal, exception, and failure scenarios on her own in an approved build, and the approved evaluator records the competence decision.
  3. Record the missing environment as a risk with an owner in the environment plan. Ask the vendor about a sandbox or second tenant, then propose compensating controls such as clearly named test patients. The client's project owner accepts the remaining risk, with privacy and security input.
  4. Record it as a new request for the change process and continue the agenda. A new request is not a misunderstanding of the sale, so it does not need commercial resolution in the meeting.
  5. It is a remembered commitment, because it is not in a signed document. Log it for verification with an owner, such as the sales representative, and a due date. It is not scope until someone with authority confirms it.
