Module 11, Lesson 1: Walk the whole operating day

Walk the whole operating day

The parts were built and tested one module at a time. Problems now hide in the gaps between them, where a task leaves one team and no one picks it up.

By now the electronic health record (EHR) system is the practice's operational record. Some call it the practice's operational brain. Work, reminders, follow-up, measures, and evidence no longer depend on one person's memory. People still own clinical, financial, security, and business judgment.

Walk the work from start to finish with the people who will run it. Use one patient journey:

inquiry or referral → scheduling → intake → identity → arrival → visit → documentation → orders or tasks → billing → payment → follow-up → records and reporting

At every step, ask:
  • Who owns the next action?
  • What record proves the step is done?
  • Which information is the official version?
  • What queue or reminder shows unfinished work?
  • What permission or approval applies?
  • What happens when the normal path fails?
  • What measure shows health or backlog?
  • What does the user do during downtime?
  • Who catches up the work after recovery?

Look for seams. A seam is a point where work passes between steps or teams. Examples:

  • a task that ends in one area but never arrives in the next;
  • an exception with no queue;
  • an alert with no owner;
  • a report no one reviews;
  • a form that saves correctly but no one acts on.

Walk at least two normal journeys and two failure journeys, such as a lab interface outage and a patient with two records.

Rule: Every step in the journey has a named owner for the next action.

Check yourself

  1. What is a seam, and why do problems hide there?
  2. Name five questions you ask at each step of the journey.
  3. Why do you also walk failure journeys, not only normal ones?

Walk the site

For a cloud EHR, the clinic's own equipment and network are among the most likely day-one failures. They usually belong to the clinic or its IT provider, so you must check them early enough for the owner to fix them.

With the clinic's information technology (IT) owner, visit every room where staff will use the system. Confirm that each of these works from each room:

  • workstations and laptops;
  • scanners and signature pads;
  • label printers;
  • prescription printers, including tamper-resistant paper where the clinic needs it;
  • Wi-Fi, with any weak or "dead" areas marked;
  • printing from the EHR to the right printer.

Then test the backup internet connection. Unplug the main line, or have the IT owner switch it off, and confirm the system still works.

Record each failure with an owner and a fix date. Put open items on the readiness dashboard.

Compare two findings. At Cedar Point main, a scanner in the front office fails because a driver is missing. The clinic's IT provider fixes it in a day. At Lakeshore, Wi-Fi does not reach exam room 4. Fixing it needs new hardware, so the IT owner orders it and the clinic places a wired workstation in the room for now. In both cases the clinic's owner fixes the problem, and you record it.

Rule: Test every device and room with the clinic's IT owner, and record an owner for each failure.

Check yourself

  1. Name five items you check in each room.
  2. Why should the site walk happen weeks before go-live, not the day before?
  3. The backup internet line fails the test. Who fixes it, and what do you record?

Simplify without removing controls

A late change to reduce clicks can remove a safety check without anyone noticing. Simplify, but keep every required control.

Aim for a simple flow from start to finish. Look for:

  • the same data entered twice;
  • handoffs that add nothing;
  • status values that conflict;
  • unused fields;
  • extra approvals with no control purpose;
  • work that moves between queues more than needed;
  • reports that repeat each other;
  • reminders that lead to no action;
  • training steps that exist only to work around a fixable setting.

Do not remove identity checks, permissions, privacy and security controls, clinical approvals, separation of financial duties, test-result follow-up, or audit evidence just to save clicks.

Record the reason for any late workflow change. Then rerun the affected setup tests, integrations, automation tests, training, and user acceptance before release.

Rule: Every late simplification keeps its controls and gets retested.

Check yourself

  1. Name four kinds of waste you look for when simplifying.
  2. A provider asks you to remove the second check on abnormal results to save time. What would you do?
  3. Why must a late workflow change be retested?

Make reminders and queues accountable

An alert that nobody owns is only noise. A queue with no aging rule can hide unfinished work for weeks.

For every queue, alert, notice, or reminder, record:

  • trigger;
  • receiving role;
  • expected response;
  • due date or aging rule (how long an item may wait);
  • escalation;
  • backup owner;
  • proof that the item is closed;
  • what happens during downtime;
  • monitoring measure.

A reminder that cannot be closed makes it hard to tell unfinished work from old notices.

Check the volume under realistic conditions. A control that sends too many low-value alerts teaches users to ignore the important ones. The owner of each area approves its thresholds and escalation.

> **Rule:** Every queue has an owner, a backup, an aging rule, and an escalation path.

Check yourself

  1. Name six fields you record for every queue or reminder.
  2. Why can too many alerts be as risky as too few?
  3. A refill queue has an owner but no aging rule. Who approves the rule, and what do you do?

Put quality measures inside the workflow

A measure helps only if someone acts on it. And after go-live, the clinic can show improvement only if it measured where it started.

Choose measures that help run the system, not measures that only decorate a dashboard. Examples:

  • incomplete intake items;
  • possible duplicate patient records;
  • unsigned or unreviewed notes;
  • overdue results, tasks, or deadlines;
  • rejected or pending transactions;
  • unresolved conversion exceptions;
  • failed interface messages;
  • corrections or overrides of automation;
  • training follow-up;
  • open defects by severity;
  • support requests and their age;
  • time through a key workflow.

For each measure, define:

  • its purpose;
  • the exact numerator, denominator, population, and time period;
  • source;
  • owner;
  • review schedule;
  • threshold;
  • action when the measure is outside the threshold;
  • evidence and how long it is kept.

Clinical quality, payer, financial, and regulatory measures need a qualified person to define and review them. You make sure the approved definition is built correctly and can be traced to source data.

Capture the baseline now

A baseline is the value of a measure before go-live. Capture the pre-live baseline for each agreed measure now. Use the same definitions you will use after go-live. If no baseline is possible, record that and the reason.

Without a baseline, the 30-, 60-, and 90-day reviews in Module 13 cannot show whether anything improved.

Rule: Capture each measure's baseline before go-live, using the same definition you will use after.

Check yourself

  1. Name five parts of a measure definition.
  2. Why must the baseline use the same definition as the post-live measure?
  3. The old system cannot report time to sign notes. What do you record?

Build audit-ready evidence

Months later, someone will ask why a setting was approved or how a defect was closed. If the answer lives in personal email, nobody can find it.

Audit-ready means the clinic can find complete, clear evidence for an approved purpose. It should not have to rebuild the project from memory. It does not mean every audit will pass, and you should never promise that.

Assemble:

  • the controlling scope and approved changes;
  • decisions and who made them;
  • workflow and setup versions;
  • access approvals;
  • conversion mapping, reconciliation, and acceptance;
  • integration and automation tests;
  • training and competence records;
  • quality control (QC) results, defects, retests, and release decisions;
  • recovery objectives, restore tests, and security evidence;
  • readiness and cutover evidence;
  • the operational owner and support handoff.

Then test retrieval. Ask someone who did not build the record to find the evidence for a requirement, an approval, a defect closure, or a converted patient group. If that person cannot follow the trail, the record is not ready.

Rule: Evidence is ready only when someone who did not build it can find it.

Check yourself

  1. What does "audit-ready" mean, and what does it not mean?
  2. Why does the builder of the index not count as a fair retrieval test?
  3. A colleague cannot find the approval for a converted patient group. What do you do?
