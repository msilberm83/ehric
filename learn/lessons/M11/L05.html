Module 11, Lesson 5: Worked example: clinic readiness

Worked example: clinic readiness

A four-provider internal medicine clinic has finished its modules, training, and conversion tests. Here is how the implementation specialist prepared the go/no-go package.

  1. Did: built the readiness dashboard by area. Produced: a dashboard showing 95 percent of tasks done and two areas not ready. Why: one overall figure would have hidden the two open areas.
  2. Did: reviewed the queue register. The abnormal-results queue had no aging rule or escalation. Produced: a register finding marked as a patient-safety blocker. Why: an unread abnormal result could sit for days.
  3. Did: drafted an aging and escalation rule and took it to the clinical lead, who approved it. The team built and tested it. Produced: an approved rule and a passed test. Why: the clinical lead owns clinical thresholds; the specialist drafts and builds.

Prompt 1: Why did the dashboard mark patient safety as not ready even though most tasks were done?

  1. Did: ran the timed cutover rehearsal. The final data load took nine hours, and the approved window was six. Produced: a rehearsal record with the overrun marked. Why: timing problems must show up in rehearsal, not on go-live night.
  2. Did: set out options to the data owner. The data owner approved loading older scanned documents in a later wave, with a written plan for where staff find them meanwhile. Produced: a recorded decision and a staff instruction. Why: what data is available on day one is the data owner's decision.

Prompt 2: Why did the specialist bring options to the data owner instead of choosing one?

  1. Did: ran a second rehearsal with the smaller load. Produced: a second record showing the load fit the window. Why: a plan change needs proof, not an estimate.
  2. Did: got clinical and QA approval of both changes, updated the dashboard, and presented the package and a "go" recommendation to the named leaders. Produced: an approved package and a recorded go decision. Why: the authorized leaders decide; the specialist records it.

Prompt 3: Why did the team run a second rehearsal instead of trusting the estimate for the smaller load?

Field assignment

Prepare the go-live readiness package using the Cedar Point Internal Medicine practice case. Do not use a real client, employer, or patient, even with names removed.

  1. Walk two normal journeys and two failure journeys.
  2. Walk both sites and record equipment and network results with owners.
  3. Review every queue, reminder, measure, and escalation owner.
  4. Record the pre-live baseline for each agreed measure.
  5. Test retrieval of the implementation and audit evidence.
  6. Draft the recovery record and list the owners who must approve each RTO and RPO.
  7. Build the security readiness checklist under the six CSF 2.0 functions.
  8. Build the onsite and remote support roster, with super users by location and shift.
  9. Record the clinic's go-live schedule decision.
  10. Write the command, communication, triage, and decision procedures, and the patient and partner notice list.
  11. Rehearse cutover, a serious failure, and a full patient visit.
  12. Run the final non-production QA review, including user sign-in and EPCS status.
  13. Check that each user's training is current.
  14. Complete the readiness dashboard.
  15. Hold a role-played go/no-go meeting and record the decision.

Self-check rubric. Your package is ready when:

Knowledge check

  1. What does it mean for the system to hold the clinic's operational work without replacing human judgment?
  2. What information must every queue or reminder have?
  3. Why can percent complete be misleading?
  4. What is the difference between an RTO and an RPO, and who approves them?
  5. Under which CSF 2.0 function would you place audit-log review, and under which would you place a restore test?
  6. What is the status of the proposed HIPAA Security Rule update, and how should it affect go-live criteria?
  7. What is the purpose of the final integrated QA review?
  8. What should a cutover rehearsal test besides the normal sequence?
  9. Who assembles the go/no-go facts, and who makes the decision?

Spaced review

  1. (Module 10) A week before go-live, the vendor moves Cedar Point's AI document sorter to a new model version. What do you do before staff use it?
  2. (Module 10) The clinical lead wants a certified predictive risk score turned on at go-live. What do you collect, and who decides?
  3. (Module 8) Staff will keep booking appointments in the old system after the final test conversion. What must the cutover plan say about this work?
  4. (Module 7) A fix for a label-printing defect goes in two days before final QA. What testing must follow besides retesting the fix?
  5. (Module 2) The go-live date is written in the signed contract, and the clinic now wants to move it. How is this different from moving a target date?

Module 11 gate — authorized to go live

Who must be in the room: the client sponsor, the clinical lead, the security official, and the named go/no-go decision-makers.

Evidence to keep: journey review, site record, operational-control and baseline record, audit index, recovery record, security checklist, support roster, schedule decision, command and communication plan, rehearsal records, final QA report, training roster, readiness dashboard, and go/no-go decision. This evidence supports your practice work. The EHRIC capstone itself is the three-artifact project described in the capstone candidate packet.
