Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Walk the whole operating day. 1. A seam is a point where work passes between steps or teams. Problems hide there because each side assumes the other owns the work. 2. Any five from the list: who owns the next action, what record proves completion, which information is official, what queue shows unfinished work, what permission applies, what happens on failure, what measure shows backlog, what users do in downtime, who catches up afterward. 3. Failures are when work is most likely to be lost, and the normal path will not reveal missing exception queues or downtime steps.

Walk the site. 1. Any five: workstations, scanners, signature pads, label printers, prescription printers, Wi-Fi, and printing from the EHR. 2. The clinic or its IT provider may need to order hardware or fix the network, which takes time. 3. The clinic's IT owner or provider fixes it. You record the failure, the owner, and the fix date, and show it on the dashboard.

Simplify without removing controls. 1. Any four: duplicate entry, empty handoffs, conflicting statuses, unused fields, extra approvals, extra queue moves, repeated reports, reminders with no action, training workarounds. 2. Keep the check; it is a test-result follow-up control. Take the request to the clinical lead and look for ways to simplify that keep the control. 3. A change can break setup, integrations, automation, or training that already passed.

Make reminders and queues accountable. 1. Any six: trigger, receiving role, expected response, aging rule, escalation, backup owner, closure proof, downtime behavior, monitoring measure. 2. Users learn to ignore alerts, so the important ones are missed. 3. The clinical owner of the area approves it. You draft the rule and take it for approval before go-live.

Put quality measures inside the workflow. 1. Any five: purpose, numerator and denominator, population and period, source, owner, review schedule, threshold, action, evidence. 2. Otherwise, a change in the number may come from the definition, not from a real change in the work. 3. Record that no baseline is possible and why, so later reviews know the starting point is missing.

Build audit-ready evidence. 1. The clinic can find complete, clear evidence for an approved purpose. It does not mean every audit will pass. 2. The builder knows where things are, so they cannot show whether someone else can follow the trail. 3. Fix the gap: add or link the missing approval, then repeat the retrieval test.

Set recovery objectives and get them approved. 1. The RTO is how long a system can stay in recovery before harm. The RPO is how much recent data can be lost. 2. Show the gap and the options (higher tier, manual workaround, accept the gap) to the named owners and record their written decision. 3. Patient safety is the clinical authority's decision, and the sponsor may not have the clinical knowledge to weigh the risk.

Check security readiness against a recognized framework. 1. Audit-log review goes under Detect. A restore test goes under Recover. 2. No. Logs nobody reviews detect nothing. Ask the security official to name a reviewer and keep Detect open until it is recorded. 3. The clinic's security owner.

Define go-live support. 1. Any five: location or channel, hours, authority, issue types, escalation, contact method, backup, evidence and handoff. 2. Peers need help where and when they work; a super user who works mornings at one site cannot help the afternoon shift at another. 3. Extend Lakeshore coverage to closing time, name a backup, and update the roster. This is your own roster to fix.

Agree the go-live schedule plan. 1. How much to reduce volume, for which providers and roles, for how long, and the date normal volume resumes. 2. The clinic's operations owner. 3. Present what similar clinics needed and other options, such as extra staff support per provider. Ask the operations owner to decide and record the decision.

Build the command and communication plan. 1. So one owner works the problem with all the evidence, and teams do not work against each other. 2. Any three: labs, pharmacies, billing services, referral partners. 3. The clinic.

Rehearse cutover and the first operating day. 1. A serious but likely failure, such as an interface outage, a delayed conversion, or a security incident. 2. Users need to practice on the screens they will actually use; an older release may differ. 3. Schedule the failure rehearsal before the go/no-go meeting and record it. This is your own work.

Run final QA on the non-production system. 1. Each module already had its own QC; final QA checks that all parts work together. 2. Identity proofing, a two-factor credential, two-person access setup, and the application's audit or certification report. 3. Record the status, tell the clinic, and ask it to plan for that prescriber on day one, such as another prescriber for controlled substances until the step is complete.

Confirm training is current. 1. Users trained on an older release and users trained long ago. 2. A mark from months ago, or on a changed release, does not show readiness for the live system. 3. The approved evaluator.

Build the readiness dashboard. 1. One figure can hide a single critical failure. 2. Ready criteria, evidence, owner, status, blocking item, and the area itself. 3. The open must-pass restore test, with its owner and date.

Offer a pre-live SAFER review. 1. High Priority Practices, or targeted reviews of Contingency Planning, Patient Identification, and Test Results Reporting and Follow-Up. 2. The guide is the clinic's self-assessment. You bring evidence; the clinic judges its own practices.

Make the go/no-go decision. 1. Go as planned, go with an approved limit, phased go-live, delay, and no-go until conditions are met. 2. The authorized client and vendor leaders decide. You assemble the facts. 3. Do not announce. Present the package to the named decision-makers and record their decision.

Worked example prompts

Prompt 1. A must-pass item was open. An unread abnormal result can harm a patient, no matter how many other tasks are done.

Prompt 2. What data staff can see on day one affects care and operations, so the data owner decides. The specialist sets out options and consequences.

Prompt 3. Estimates were what failed the first time. Only a timed rehearsal shows the new load fits the window.

Faded example

  1. Produced: a recorded schedule decision from the clinic's operations owner: how far to reduce visits, for which providers, for how long, and the date full volume resumes. Why: the clinic owns its schedule; you supply what similar clinics needed.
  2. Produced: a notice list covering patients (signs, phone message, portal or text notice) and partners (labs, pharmacies, billing service, referral partners), with the clinic's approved patient wording. Why: patients and partners must not be surprised by delays or new routes for orders and claims.
  3. Produced: a QA record showing each user signed in with their own account and the right role, and each EPCS prescriber's identity proofing, two-factor credential, and two-person access setup, plus the application report. Why: failed sign-ins are common day-one problems, and a prescriber who is not EPCS-ready cannot e-prescribe controlled substances.
  4. Produced: an offer of a High Priority Practices review, or targeted reviews, with project evidence attached. The clinic's team rates. Why: it catches safety gaps and gives Module 13 a starting point.
  5. Produced: a package with the dashboard by area, open blockers and owners, and a recommendation, presented to the named leaders, with their decision recorded. Why: the authorized leaders decide; you assemble the facts and record the outcome.

Knowledge check

  1. The system holds the work, reminders, follow-up, measures, and evidence, so they no longer depend on one person's memory. People still make clinical, financial, security, and business judgments. The system coordinates; it does not decide.
  2. Every queue or reminder needs a trigger, receiving role, expected response, aging rule, escalation, backup owner, closure proof, downtime behavior, and a monitoring measure. The owner of the area approves thresholds and escalation.
  3. One overall figure can look high while a single must-pass item, such as a restore test, is open. A dashboard by area names each blocker and its owner, so leaders see what actually stops go-live.
  4. The RTO is how long a system can stay in recovery before the outage harms the clinic's work. The RPO is the point to which data must be recovered, or how much recent work may be lost. The owners named in the governance record approve them in writing, with patient-safety gaps going to the clinical authority.
  5. Audit-log review belongs under Detect, because it is how misuse is found. A restore test belongs under Recover, because it shows operations can be restored.
  6. HHS proposed the update on January 6, 2025. As of September 30, 2026, it was still a proposal, and the current Security Rule applies. A proposed practice, such as a 72-hour restore, can become a go-live criterion only if the security official chooses it, recorded as the client's own objective, not as law.
  7. It is the final check that all the parts work together on the approved non-production version: build, data, access, integrations, automation, queues, downtime materials, defects, and training version. It follows each module's own QC, and it now includes user sign-in and EPCS readiness.
  8. It should test a serious but likely failure, such as a delayed conversion, an interface outage, an access problem, a network outage, or a security incident. It should also include a user rehearsal of a full patient visit in the release going live.
  9. The implementation specialist assembles the facts and the recommendation. The authorized client and vendor leaders named in the governance record make the decision, and the specialist records it.

Spaced review

  1. Rerun the approved evaluation set on the new version, compare the results by category with the thresholds set in advance, and record the version and results. The owner decides whether the new version may be used.
  2. Collect the source attributes, copy them into the inventory, and flag gaps such as a model built for hospital patients. The client's decision-support or AI governance owner decides whether, where, and for whom to turn it on.
  3. It must define how that work reaches the new system: a final delta conversion, controlled manual entry, a limited dual-entry period, or a freeze. Each option needs an owner and reconciliation.
  4. Regression testing. Choose a risk-based set of tests that could be affected by the change and rerun them, so the fix does not break something that passed before.
  5. A contractual date is in an executed agreement, so moving it may need escalation or a formal change. A target date is only desired and can be adjusted through normal planning.
