Module 11, Lesson 4: Rehearse cutover and the first operating day

Rehearse cutover and the first operating day

A rehearsal finds the step that takes too long or depends on someone who is missing. It is far cheaper to find that now than at 6 a.m. on go-live day.

Cutover is the move from the old system to the new one. Run a tabletop or hands-on rehearsal of these steps:

  1. final change and data freeze;
  2. final data pull or setup promotion;
  3. load and reconciliation;
  4. interface and device activation;
  5. access check;
  6. critical workflow quick tests;
  7. go/no-go review;
  8. user communication;
  9. start of operations;
  10. issue triage;
  11. downtime or rollback decision;
  12. end-of-day reconciliation.

Time each step, find dependencies, and record corrections. Then rehearse a serious but likely failure, such as a delayed conversion, a down interface, an access problem, wrong routing, a network outage, ransomware, or a critical defect.

Include a user rehearsal

Staff in each role run a full patient visit, from scheduling to checkout, in the release going live. This is a mock go-live. It shows whether people, not only systems, are ready.

Rule: Rehearse the normal cutover, a serious failure, and a full patient visit before the go/no-go meeting.

Example A. In the Cedar Point failure rehearsal, the team practices a lab interface outage. Nobody knows who may declare downtime. The team names that person and runs the drill again. Example B. In a family clinic's user rehearsal, a medical assistant cannot print a visit summary at checkout. The printer setting is fixed before go-live day. In both cases, the rehearsal found a problem while there was still time to fix it.

Check yourself

  1. What should a cutover rehearsal test besides the normal sequence?
  2. Why does a user rehearsal use the release going live?
  3. The normal cutover fits the window, but the failure scenario was not run. What would you do?

Run final QA on the non-production system

A failed sign-in or an unready prescriber can stop work on day one. Final QA is the last chance to find such problems before real patients depend on the system.

Before go-live, quality assurance (QA) reviews the non-production system one more time. This is the final check that all the parts work together, after each module's own QC.

Make the review traceable and based on risk. Confirm:

  • the approved build and setup version;
  • required data and relationships;
  • access and role behavior;
  • key forms, templates, and codes;
  • integrations and devices;
  • automation and AI setup;
  • queues, reminders, reports, and audit;
  • downtime materials;
  • defects and accepted limits;
  • training version;
  • cutover and support plan.

Check every user's access

Every user has signed in with their own account and confirmed the correct role. Failed sign-ins are among the most common day-one problems.

Check prescribing readiness

Electronic prescribing of controlled substances (EPCS) has extra federal rules from the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). For each prescriber who will e-prescribe controlled substances, confirm:

  • identity proofing is complete;
  • the prescriber has a two-factor credential;
  • two people have set up the prescriber's access, as the DEA rule requires;
  • the vendor has the third-party audit or certification report that the DEA rule requires for the application.

The prescriber and the clinic complete these steps. You confirm and record the status. A prescriber who is not ready cannot e-prescribe controlled substances on day one, so the clinic needs a plan for them.

Rule: Final QA passes only when every user has signed in with the right role and every EPCS prescriber is ready or has a plan.

Example A. At Cedar Point, 41 of 42 users sign in with the right role. One front-desk user sees the billing menu. The role is corrected, and she signs in again before QA passes. Example B. At a family clinic, each of the 3 prescribers who will send controlled-substance prescriptions shows identity proofing, a two-factor credential, and two-person access setup. You record each status in the QA report. In both cases, QA checked each person, not only the system.

Check yourself

  1. Why is final QA not the first time QA sees the system?
  2. Name the four EPCS items you confirm for each prescriber.
  3. One provider's EPCS identity proofing is still pending a week before go-live. What would you do?

Confirm training is current

Training given months ago, or on screens that later changed, does not prepare users for go-live. A "competent" record must refer to the build that will actually go live.

Check the training roster. Every user has shown competence on the release going live. Anyone trained on an older release, or long ago, gets a short refresher on the changed workflows. For providers, final training usually falls in the last two to four weeks.

Compare two users. A nurse trained six weeks ago on the current release passes a short skills check and needs nothing more. A provider trained four months ago, before the results screens changed, gets a one-hour refresher on results and a new skills check.

Rule: Competence counts only on the release that will go live.

Check yourself

  1. Which users need a refresher before go-live?
  2. Why does a training date matter, not only a "competent" mark?
  3. Who records the new competence decision after a refresher?

Build the readiness dashboard

A single "96 percent complete" figure can hide one failure that blocks go-live. Leaders need to see each area and each blocker.

Use readiness areas, not one overall percent:

Area Ready criteria Evidence Owner Status Blocking item
Scope and decisions
Workflow and configuration
Data conversion
Integrations and devices
Site equipment and network
Automation and AI
Privacy and security
Backup, restore, and recovery objectives
Training and staffing
QC and user acceptance
Cutover and downtime
Support and communications

The dashboard names blockers and the people who can decide on them.

Rule: Report readiness by area, and lead with any must-pass blocker.

Example A. At Cedar Point, 11 areas are ready. Privacy and security has one open must-pass item: no one is named to review the audit log. You put that row first, with the security official as owner. Example B. A dermatology clinic has 3 open items. Each has an approved limit, and none is must-pass. You report those areas as ready with limits. In both cases, leaders see first whatever could stop go-live.

Check yourself

  1. Why can percent complete be misleading?
  2. What six columns does each dashboard row have?
  3. Training is 41 of 42 done, and the restore test is open and must-pass. What do you report first?

Offer a pre-live SAFER review

A short safety self-check before go-live can catch gaps that the project view misses. Module 13 repeats this check after go-live, so a pre-live result also gives a starting point.

The SAFER Guides (Safety Assurance Factors for EHR Resilience) are free self-assessment checklists from ONC. Offer the clinic's team a pre-live review using the High Priority Practices guide. Or offer targeted reviews using the Contingency Planning, Patient Identification, and Test Results Reporting and Follow-Up guides.

The clinic's team does the rating. You bring evidence from the project, such as the recovery record, the queue register, and test results.

Rule: The clinic rates its SAFER self-assessment; you bring the evidence.

Example A. Cedar Point's clinical lead and practice administrator rate the High Priority Practices guide. You bring the recovery record and the queue register. Example B. A pediatric clinic chooses only the Patient Identification guide. Its office manager rates it, and you bring the duplicate-record test results. In both cases, the clinic's team did the rating, and you supplied the evidence.

Check yourself

  1. Which SAFER guides fit a pre-live review?
  2. Why do you not rate the guide yourself?

Make the go/no-go decision

The go-live decision commits patients, staff, and money. It must rest on agreed criteria and be made by the people with authority.

Define the participants and criteria in advance. A go decision should confirm that:

  • must-pass requirements are met;
  • no open defect breaks the approved release rule;
  • data and interfaces meet acceptance;
  • access, security, privacy, and clinical approvals are complete;
  • recovery objectives are approved and a restore test supports them;
  • site equipment and network work;
  • users have current training, and support staff are ready;
  • the schedule plan is agreed, and patients and partners are notified;
  • downtime and recovery steps are usable;
  • cutover fits the available window;
  • open limits are understood and accepted by the proper authority;
  • success and pause criteria are clear.

Possible decisions are:

  • go as planned;
  • go with an approved limit and an active control;
  • phased go-live;
  • delay;
  • no-go until named conditions are met.

You assemble the facts and the recommendation. The authorized client and vendor leaders decide under the approved governance.

Rule: You assemble the facts; the authorized leaders decide, and you record it.

Example A. At Cedar Point, you present the dashboard, the rehearsal records, and a "go" recommendation. The sponsor and the clinical lead decide "go." You record their names and the time. Example B. At a family clinic, the interface to a small reference lab is not ready. You recommend a phased go-live. The authorized leaders choose to go with an approved limit: staff send those orders on paper until the interface passes. You record the decision. In both cases, you supplied the facts, and the leaders decided.

Check yourself

  1. Name the five possible decisions.
  2. Who makes the go/no-go decision, and who assembles the facts?
  3. A vendor sales manager tells you to announce go-live today. What would you do?
