Module 4, Lesson 1: Start by asking to see real work

Start by asking to see real work

Discovery is the part of the project where you learn how the client's work happens today. It turns purchased features into decisions about how people will work. If you design from a wish list, you build an ideal process that nobody follows.

A workflow is the series of steps people take to finish one piece of work, such as checking in a patient. The software can provide appointment types, visit templates, routing rules, forms, task lists, and permissions. The client still has to decide how its people will use them.

The central discovery question is:

"Show me how this work happens today."

Ask the person who does the work to walk through a real, typical example. Watch the order of steps, the information, the handoffs, the decisions, the exceptions, the delays, and the workarounds. Ask what happens before and after the part you are discussing. The answer often crosses departments and systems.

Do not begin with "What do you want the new system to do?" That question usually produces a feature wish list or an idealized process. First understand the actual work. Then design the future with the client.

Discovery is not a single interview with a department head. The head can explain policy and approve the future workflow. The people doing the work can show where policy, habit, and reality differ. Use both views.

If you have worked a front desk, you know the written procedure and the real one are rarely the same.

Rule: Ask the people who do the work to show you a real example before anyone lists features.

Compare two cases. At Lakeshore, you ask a front-desk worker to show you one real check-in. She shows that she types paper forms into the old system by hand. At a pediatric clinic, a nurse walks you through one real vaccine visit. She shows a paper log she keeps because the old system cannot print the record parents ask for. The shared rule: a real example shows work that no feature list mentions.

Check yourself

  1. What is the central discovery question?
  2. Why does "What do you want the new system to do?" make a poor first question?
  3. A department head offers to describe the whole referral process for you. What would you do?

Plan discovery by end-to-end workflow

Sessions organized by product menu make people talk about screens. Sessions organized by end-to-end workflow show the handoffs, where most failures happen.

An end-to-end workflow runs from the event that starts the work (the trigger) to the finished result. Healthcare examples:

  • referral or inquiry to scheduled appointment;
  • intake to arrival and patient identification;
  • check-in to rooming or waiting;
  • clinical documentation to orders and follow-up;
  • charge capture to claim, rejection, payment, and patient balance;
  • test result to review, communication, and closure;
  • record request to authorized release.

For each session, identify:

  • scope, and the start and end points;
  • participants and the decision authority needed;
  • sample scenarios;
  • known pain points and past incidents;
  • documents or approved policies to review;
  • decisions expected from the session;
  • the facilitator, the note-taker, and the follow-up owner.

Use made-up or properly protected examples. Do not show protected health information (PHI) (health information that identifies a patient), passwords, or live data exports in an open workshop or course workbook.

Rule: Plan each discovery session around one workflow from trigger to outcome, not around a product menu.

Compare two plans. One clinic plans a session called "Scheduling module." People spend the hour on screen settings. Another clinic plans "Referral to scheduled appointment." The group finds that faxed referrals wait two days in a tray no one owns. The shared rule: following the work from start to finish exposes the handoff that fails.

Check yourself

  1. List five things to identify for each discovery session.
  2. Why does an end-to-end session find problems that a menu-based session misses?
  3. A participant wants to bring a real patient chart to a workshop "to show the problem." What would you do?

Map the current state

The current state is how work happens today. A map of it lets people who were not in the room check it and build from it. If only the attendees can read the map, it cannot guide the design.

A useful map shows:

  • the trigger that starts the workflow;
  • roles or teams in separate lanes;
  • actions in order;
  • information created or changed;
  • systems, forms, messages, or devices used;
  • decision points and their criteria;
  • handoffs;
  • waits and queues;
  • normal completion;
  • important exceptions;
  • controls, approvals, and evidence.

A swimlane map puts each role in its own row, or lane, so you can see each handoff between roles. Simple shapes are usually enough:

  • rounded box: start or end;
  • rectangle: task;
  • diamond: decision;
  • document symbol: form or record;
  • arrow: flow or handoff;
  • dotted line: outside dependency;
  • note: risk, policy, control, or open question.

Use the same shapes every time and include a legend. The goal is shared understanding, not a perfect diagram.

Rule: A current-state map must make sense to someone who was not in the meeting.

Compare two maps. At one clinic, a referral map has boxes labeled "RC" (short for referral coordinator) and "step 2" and no legend. Only the people in the room can read it. At Lakeshore, the map puts the referral coordinator and the scheduler in separate lanes. It uses a diamond for each decision and includes a legend. A billing lead who missed the session can follow it. The shared rule: lanes, standard shapes, and a legend let other people check the map.

Check yourself

  1. List six things a useful current-state map shows.
  2. Why does a map need a legend?
  3. Who should validate a current-state map before you design from it?

Ask for the exceptions

The normal path is usually easy. Failures appear in the exceptions: the cases that do not follow the normal path. If you design only the normal path, staff invent their own fixes on day one.

Ask:

  • What if required information is missing?
  • What if the person cannot be matched to an existing record?
  • What if a signature is refused or cannot be obtained?
  • What if an order, result, document, or claim is not returned?
  • What if the interface (an automatic link to another system) is down?
  • What if the staff member lacks access?
  • What if the decision-maker is absent?
  • What if a deadline changes?
  • What happens during downtime, when the system is unavailable?
  • Who notices that the work stalled?
  • Who has authority to override or reroute it?

Record the approved path for each important exception.

Rule: "Staff will figure it out" is not a process; record the approved exception path.

Compare two cases. At one clinic, the lab interface fails and nobody knows who checks for missing results, so a result sits unread for a week. At another, the map says the nurse lead checks a daily "results not received" list. The shared rule: every exception needs a named path and someone who notices when work stalls.

Check yourself

  1. List five exception questions to ask in discovery.
  2. Why is "staff will figure it out" a risk to patient safety?
  3. During mapping, nobody can say who notices a referral that was never scheduled. What do you do?

Separate policy from habit

Staff often say "We have to do it this way." If you treat a habit as law, you build needless steps. If you drop a real rule, you build something unsafe or noncompliant.

When participants say "We have to," ask what creates the requirement. It may come from law, a professional rule, a payer requirement, a contract, accreditation, organizational policy, product limits, or habit.

Do not challenge a real requirement casually. Do not treat a long habit as a legal requirement without checking. Record the source and the person responsible for confirming it.

Rule: For every "we have to," record the source and the person who confirms it.

Compare two cases. A medical assistant says a second signature on every injection log is "the law." No one knows the source, so you record it as unconfirmed and ask the compliance lead. A billing clerk says a payer requires a referral number on certain claims, and shows the payer's written rule. You record it as a payer requirement with its source. The shared rule: the source decides, not the confidence of the speaker.

Check yourself

  1. Name five possible sources of a "must."
  2. Why is removing an unexplained step just as risky as keeping it?
  3. A nurse says a step is required by accreditation, but cannot say which standard. What do you record?

Write requirements with evidence

A vague requirement such as "configure check-in" cannot be built, tested, or approved. A clear one can be traced from the sale to go-live.

A requirement is a statement of what the system and the people must do. A useful one states:

  • who needs the capability;
  • what must happen;
  • under what condition;
  • what control applies;
  • how the team will check it.

Example:

When front-desk staff begin check-in, the system must require the approved patient-identification steps before the visit can proceed. The clinical safety owner approves the steps. User acceptance testing will include a correct match, a likely duplicate, and a mismatch.

User acceptance testing (UAT) is testing by the client's own users to confirm the system supports the approved workflow.

Keep traceability: a link from each requirement back to the sale and forward to its proof.

purchased outcome → workflow requirement → configuration item → training objective → test case → approval

Traceability lets you prove that the build still matches the sale and the approved workflow after hundreds of small decisions.

Rule: Every requirement names who, what, when, the control, and how it will be checked.

Compare two drafts. "The system should make abnormal results easier to find" has no owner, condition, or test. "Abnormal lab results go to the ordering provider's inbox; the medical director approves; UAT tests normal, abnormal, and unassigned results" can be built and checked. The shared rule: if you cannot test it, it is not a requirement yet.

Check yourself

  1. List the five parts of a useful requirement.
  2. Why does traceability matter after hundreds of small decisions?
  3. Rewrite "configure the result inbox" as a requirement.
