Module 4 — Answers: Check yourself, examples, knowledge check, spaced review

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Start by asking to see real work

  1. "Show me how this work happens today."
  2. It produces a feature wish list or an ideal process, not the real work with its handoffs, exceptions, and workarounds.
  3. Use the head for policy and approval, but also ask the people who do the referrals to show real examples.

Plan discovery by end-to-end workflow

  1. Any five of: scope and start and end points; participants and decision authority; sample scenarios; pain points and past incidents; documents or policies; expected decisions; facilitator, note-taker, and follow-up owner.
  2. Following the work from trigger to outcome crosses roles and systems, so it shows the handoffs where work is lost. A menu session stays inside one screen.
  3. Decline the real chart. Use a made-up or properly protected example that shows the same problem.

Map the current state

  1. Any six of: trigger; roles in lanes; actions in order; information created or changed; systems, forms, or devices; decision points; handoffs; waits and queues; normal completion; exceptions; controls and evidence.
  2. A legend lets people who were not in the meeting read the shapes the same way.
  3. The people who do the work and the people who own it.

Ask for the exceptions

  1. Any five of the questions in the section, such as missing information, no record match, refused signature, interface down, absent decision-maker, or downtime.
  2. Each person invents a different fix, often under pressure. Work stalls with no one noticing, and results or referrals can be lost.
  3. Record it as an open question and ask the workflow owner to name who monitors unscheduled referrals and how.

Separate policy from habit

  1. Any five of: law, professional rule, payer requirement, contract, accreditation, organizational policy, product limit, or habit.
  2. The step may protect a real legal or safety need. Removing it without checking can make the new workflow unsafe or noncompliant.
  3. Record the step as unconfirmed, with "accreditation" as the claimed source, and ask the accountable compliance or quality owner to confirm the standard by a date.

Write requirements with evidence

  1. Who needs it; what must happen; under what condition; what control applies; how it will be checked.
  2. It lets you prove that the build still matches the sale and the approved workflow, and shows what a change affects.
  3. Example: "When a result arrives, the system routes it to the ordering provider's inbox. The medical director approves the routing rules. UAT tests a normal result, an abnormal result, and a result with no ordering provider."

Ask about accessibility in discovery

  1. Level AA, which includes Level A.
  2. The ACR describes the vendor's base product. The clinic's configured forms, templates, and messages can still fail.
  3. Say you cannot state that. Only the client's authority can make a conformance claim, for a defined scope and date. Explain the open items and the testing plan.

Design the future state

  1. Confirm the outcome and start and end points; remove steps that serve no requirement; choose the official record for each piece of information; assign each task and decision to a role.
  2. Automation makes a poor process fail faster, and no one is clearly responsible for watching it.
  3. Keep it on the automation candidate list, and send the disagreement to the person with authority with a decision date.

Control access by role

  1. View, create, change, approve, export, and delete.
  2. Old access may be too wide, and it was not designed for the new workflow or approved by privacy and security.
  3. Record her request and ask what her work needs. Send the access design, including export, to the privacy and security authorities to approve.

Plan for sensitive records

  1. The client's privacy officer, with legal counsel where needed, decides how the rules apply. Clinical leaders decide note types.
  2. These rules shape note types, access, release, the portal, and interfaces. Finding them in testing means rebuilding.
  3. Record the statement, and ask the client's privacy or legal authority to confirm it. Only that authority can decide.

Keep human authority

  1. Any four of: route, remind, restrict, calculate, record.
  2. The AI tool cannot be held accountable. A named person must review and own the result where review is required.

Balance continuity and improvement

  1. Required for the new system; required for safety, law, contract, policy, or control; necessary simplification; optional improvement; future phase.
  2. Users are already learning new software. Too much change at once raises errors and slows adoption.
  3. Required for safety, confirmed by the right authority. Yes, it goes into the go-live plan.

Hand the approved design into a controlled build

  1. Approved workflow and requirements, solution blueprint, controlled build, technical acceptance, QC, stable release for training and UAT.
  2. Tracing keeps the build matched to the sale and the approved design, and shows what a later change affects.
  3. Say it is a prototype. It can help answer a design question, but it is not UAT evidence. UAT must use a QC-tested, stable release.

Plan environments when the vendor has no separate test or training copy

  1. Any four of: clearly named test patients with limited creators; keep test data out of reports, measures, interfaces, and live claims; test before real data loads; save configuration before changes; use change windows; plan cleanup; document sandbox differences.
  2. A realistic name can be mistaken for a real patient, so test data can end up in real care, reports, or claims.
  3. In the six weeks before real data loads, because production can then act as the test system with no risk to real patient records.

Validate with scenarios

  1. Normal case; incomplete or wrong-information case; exception; access or approval case; downstream handoff; downtime or integration failure.
  2. It keeps the project testing the same workflow the client approved.
  3. It shows a missing step. Update the map and requirements so the path is defined, then walk the scenario again.

Run the approval meeting

  1. Any five of: current-state map; future-state map; requirements; role and access decisions; accessibility and sensitive-record decisions; exception and downtime paths; scenarios; open questions and deferred items; effects on scope, schedule, data, integrations, training, and support.
  2. It does not show which version the person saw, or whether they had authority to approve.
  3. A written approval of v4 from the accountable owner, with the version and the date.

Worked example prompts

  1. Watching the front desk first. The manager knows the policy, but the front desk shows the real work. Watching first revealed the wrong-record risk that the written procedure did not show.
  2. Three UAT cases. Each case tests a different risk: a correct match, a likely duplicate, and a mismatch. "Test check-in" would let a tester try only the easy case.
  3. Not letting automated matching decide alone. A wrong match puts one patient's information in another patient's chart. Patient identification is a safety decision, so the approved human check stays.

Faded example

  1. Any three, such as: What if a result arrives for a patient with no ordering provider? What if the interface is down? What if the reviewing cardiologist is absent? Who notices a result that nobody reviewed?
  2. Example: "When an abnormal result arrives, the system routes it to the ordering cardiologist's inbox and flags it. The medical director approves the routing and flag rules. UAT tests a normal result, an abnormal result, and a result with no ordering provider."
  3. Optional improvement or future phase. It is helpful but not needed for go-live, and no authority has confirmed it as required.
  4. Any four, such as: a normal result; an abnormal result; a result with no ordering provider; a result for a patient with no follow-up visit; the reviewer absent; interface downtime.
  5. The accountable owner's name, the map version approved, and the date.

Knowledge check

  1. The people who do the work can show the real sequence, handoffs, exceptions, and workarounds. A wish list shows only an ideal, so a design built from it may not fit how work really happens. Understand the real work first, then design the future with the client.
  2. It shows the trigger, roles in lanes, actions in order, information, systems, decisions, handoffs, waits, normal completion, exceptions, and controls. It uses consistent shapes and a legend. Someone who did not attend can then read it.
  3. The implementer asks what creates each "must": law, professional rule, payer, contract, accreditation, policy, product limit, or habit. The implementer records the source and the person who confirms it. The implementer does not drop a real requirement or treat a habit as law without checking.
  4. Traceability links each purchased outcome to its workflow requirement, configuration item, training objective, test case, and approval. It proves the build still matches the sale and the approved workflow after many small decisions. It also shows what a change affects.
  5. Record the applicable law, contract, or policy and who confirmed it; the target standard and level; the scope; vendor evidence; the validation plan; alternate access; the remediation owner; and the acceptance authority. An ACR covers only the vendor's base product. The configured forms and messages still need testing.
  6. Ask about psychotherapy notes, Part 2 status and records, consent capture and labeling, minors and personal representatives, and proxy access rules. The privacy officer decides how the rules apply, with legal counsel where needed. Clinical leaders decide note types.
  7. Keep a familiar process when changing it is not needed for go-live, so users are not overloaded while learning new software. Do not keep it when it is unsafe, noncompliant, or clearly broken. Sort each change into the five classes to decide.
  8. Failures happen in exceptions and downtime, not on the normal path. Without these scenarios, staff invent their own fixes on day one. Walking them before approval finds missing steps while they are cheap to fix.
  9. You need system and organizational boundaries, roles and access, official data and information flows, the items to configure, and nonfunctional requirements. You also need environments and test data, and owners for build, review, test, and support. Record decisions, assumptions, limits, and deferred work, and trace build items to approved requirements.
  10. Ask the vendor for a temporary sandbox or second tenant first. If none exists, log the risk and propose compensating controls such as clearly named test patients, excluding test data from real work, testing before real data loads, saving configuration before changes, and planned cleanup. The client's project owner accepts the remaining risk.

Spaced review

  1. As a dependent date: it depends on the lab issuing credentials, and it could move if they are late. Name the owner and the date for getting the credentials.
  2. Restate the disagreement, record the options and effects, and assign the decision to the medical director with a date. Do not settle it by vote.
  3. Work does not enter the build just because someone mentioned it. Record it as a change request, and send it through change control so its effects can be reviewed and approved.
  4. It is a remembered commitment. List it as to be confirmed, with the sales representative as owner and a due date. If sales confirms it, the commercial owner decides whether an approved change is needed.
  5. Technical or device support. Ask whether the device is supported in the target environment.
