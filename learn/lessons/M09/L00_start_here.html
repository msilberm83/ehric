Module 9 — Start here: outline and the problem

Chapter outline

  1. List every integration. Record its purpose, both endpoints, the owner on each side, and the agreement behind it. Ask about public health links. You produce an integration inventory and owner map.
  2. Draw the data flow. Show what moves, in which direction, when, and who handles failures. You produce one data-flow diagram per integration.
  3. Clear the nontechnical dependencies. Track contracts, enrollment, credentials, allowlisting, user accounts, and third-party dates. Keep secrets in the approved credential system. You produce dated dependency entries.
  4. Track e-prescribing readiness for controlled substances. For each prescriber, track identity proofing, the two-factor credential, and the two-person access setup. You produce a prescriber readiness tracker.
  5. Design acknowledgements and failure handling. Decide how each side knows an item arrived and was accepted. Decide what happens on rejection, delay, duplicates, and downtime. You produce a failure-response design.
  6. Test end to end. Run normal and failure scenarios with synthetic data. Record transport, destination, and business results separately. You produce end-to-end test and reconciliation evidence.
  7. Identify the interoperability standards in play. Name the standard and version for each clinical exchange.
  8. Route the rules to the people who decide. Check certification evidence. Send information-blocking, network, and consent questions to the client's named authorities. You produce an interoperability source register.
  9. Set up revenue-cycle transactions. Know what each claim transaction does. Keep rejections and denials apart.
  10. Track enrollment step by step. Track clearinghouse enrollment and each payer's claims, remittance, and payment enrollment separately. You produce an enrollment tracker.
  11. Govern forms, codes, and templates. Give each an owner, version, approval, and maintenance rule. You produce configuration governance records.
  12. Configure payments safely. Bring in finance and security owners, test success and failure paths, and reconcile.
  13. Hand monitoring to operations. Name who watches each queue, the thresholds, and the escalation path. You produce an operational monitoring plan.

You will produce:

  • an integration inventory, owner map, and data-flow diagrams;
  • dated dependency entries and a prescriber readiness tracker;
  • an interoperability source register and an enrollment tracker;
  • end-to-end test and reconciliation evidence;
  • configuration governance records and an operational monitoring plan.

Checked off when: every integration and configuration area has named owners, passed end-to-end and failure tests, approvals from the right authorities, and a trained operational owner.

The problem

Cedar Point Internal Medicine goes live in five weeks. The vendor's interface lead reports that "all integrations are green." Every test message to the lab, the clearinghouse, and the pharmacy network returned "accepted."

You look closer. In the lab test, two results for a synthetic patient filed to a different patient with the same name. One payer has approved claims but not electronic payment details. Dr. Okafor, a physician who treats chronic pain, has no credential yet to send controlled-substance prescriptions electronically. Nobody at the clinic has been named to watch the unmatched-results queue.

Each connection works. The work behind it does not.

Before you read on: If every test message returned "accepted," what could still be wrong? Who must finish the steps that let Dr. Okafor send controlled-substance prescriptions electronically?
