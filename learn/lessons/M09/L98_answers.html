Module 9 — Answers: Check yourself, examples, knowledge check, spaced review

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

How do you list every integration?

  1. Any six of: business purpose; source and destination; owner on each side; vendor and support contacts; contract or agreement; information sent and returned; trigger and frequency; identifiers and matching rules; standard and version; credentials and key owner; privacy and security needs; acknowledgements; failure behavior; monitoring owner; test environment; support plan.
  2. Public health links, such as an immunization registry or case reporting, are often missed in the order. Each needs its own registration and testing, so finding it late can delay go-live.
  3. Record the integration as not ready. Ask the client project owner to name the receiving owner. Do not name yourself.

Draw the data flow for each integration

  1. Source event, trigger, transformation, secure transport, destination processing, acknowledgement, reconciliation, exception queue.
  2. A marketing diagram shows what the product can do in general. The project record must show this clinic's systems, security boundary, timing, and the named person who owns each failure.

Clear the nontechnical dependencies

  1. Provider, receiver, need date, effect of delay, and escalation path.
  2. A shared log-in hides who did what. The audit trail can no longer show which person viewed or changed a record, and access cannot match each person's role.
  3. Store the key in the approved credential system, log only where it is stored, and tell the client's security owner. The security owner decides whether to replace the key.

Track e-prescribing readiness for controlled substances

  1. A third-party audit or certification report that each prescriber checks; identity proofing; a two-factor credential; and two-person access setup approved by a DEA registrant.
  2. Identity proofing and the credential belong to one person. Each prescriber applies and holds the token, so one prescriber can be ready while another is not.
  3. Decline. You are not a DEA registrant, and the approval must use a registrant's own two-factor credential. Show the gap on the tracker and ask the clinic to have its designated registrant approve the list.

How does each side know an item arrived?

  1. Transport (file arrived), syntax (format valid), and business (content accepted for processing).
  2. Users act on what they see. If they think "file arrived" means "accepted," they stop watching items that later fail.
  3. It should say who watches pending items, how long to wait, when to retry, and how to avoid sending a duplicate.

Test end to end, including failures

  1. Any five of: missing or invalid values; mismatched identifiers; duplicates; rejection and correction; delayed or missing response; unavailable endpoint; unauthorized access; changed or cancelled source item; downtime recovery.
  2. A message can arrive and still produce the wrong business result. Separate columns stop a delivery success from hiding a business failure.

Which interoperability standard does each exchange use?

  1. C-CDA sends whole clinical documents. FHIR sends small units called resources through web APIs.
  2. USCDI has several versions with different data elements. Without a version, you cannot tell which data the product can exchange.

Route interoperability rules to the people who decide

  1. It proves the product passed certification tests for the listed criteria and versions. It does not prove the client's setup is correct or that the client follows any rule.
  2. The client's privacy officer or compliance lead, with legal counsel.
  3. Log it as a change request. Route participation, agreements, and privacy review to the executive sponsor, privacy authority, and legal counsel. Network participation is not just a setting.

Set up revenue-cycle transactions

  1. The 270/271 pair handles eligibility. The 835 (ERA) explains payment.
  2. A 999 checks only the file's format. Individual claims may still be rejected on the 277CA.

Keep rejections and denials apart

  1. A rejection usually appears on a 277CA or clearinghouse report. A denial appears on the 835 with adjustment reason codes.
  2. They need different fixes, owners, and deadlines. Counting them together hides which work queue is behind and misleads the billing manager.

Track clearinghouse and payer enrollment separately

  1. Clearinghouse enrollment, payer claims (EDI) enrollment, ERA enrollment, and EFT enrollment.
  2. Claims go out, but remittances arrive on paper and payments may come by check. Log the gap as a dependency with the client's owner and a need date, and ask billing for an interim posting plan.

Govern electronic forms

  1. Any four of: owner and approver; version and effective date; purpose; prefill source; required fields; logic; signature rule; language needs; routing; record location; retention; change process.
  2. The clinic must be able to prove which wording a patient saw and signed. Without the version, a signed consent may not be defensible.

Control codes, values, and templates

  1. Any four of: business owner; source and version; active and inactive rules; effective dates; roles allowed to change it; downstream effect; test and approval; maintenance process.
  2. Near-duplicate values split reports and break automation that looks for one exact value.
  3. Teach that a template is a structure. The user must check that each default fits this patient before signing.

Configure payments safely

  1. Any five of: approved methods; success; decline; cancellation; timeout; duplicate prevention; partial payment; refund; correct account link; receipt; posting and reconciliation; access and audit; downtime.
  2. Payments involve money, card data, and contracts. Finance owns posting and reconciliation, and security owns how card data is protected.

Hand monitoring to operations

  1. Any five of: monitor and backup; dashboard or queue; review frequency; expected volume; error and aging limits; retry authority; vendor escalation; incident route; reconciliation; evidence; change review.
  2. The implementer leaves after go-live. A trained client owner with access and a backup keeps watching when the project ends.

Worked example prompts

  1. Why check enrollment first? A clean claim proves only that claims can go out. Payment, remittance, and EFT depend on separate enrollments, so the integration's full business result was not yet proven.
  2. Why ask the billing manager? The billing manager owns posting and reconciliation decisions. The specialist's job is to record the gap, find the owner, and get the decision on record.
  3. What could go wrong? The clinic would go live with remittances on paper and payments by check for a major payer. No one would have a plan to post them, and cash flow and reconciliation would suffer.

Faded example

  1. Clinical leadership decides. Produced: a recorded decision that results failing the birth-date match go to an unmatched-results queue for staff review. Why: filing rules for clinical data are a clinical safety decision.
  2. The vendor changes the matching rule. QC retests the near-match case and reruns the earlier normal-result tests. Produced: retest and regression evidence. Why: a fix can break cases that passed before.
  3. The practice manager names the owner and backup for the unmatched queue. Produced: an operational owner record. Why: if nobody owns the receiving queue, the interface is not ready.

Knowledge check

  1. A connection proves only that two systems can exchange data. The business workflow also needs correct meaning, matching, owners, failure handling, and reconciliation. A result can arrive and still file to the wrong patient.
  2. Transport acknowledgement says the item arrived. Business acceptance says the receiver accepted the content for processing. An item can pass transport and still be rejected, so each layer is tested and trained separately.
  3. A 999 reports whether the whole file met X12 format rules. A 277CA reports, claim by claim, whether each claim passed the payer's first checks or was rejected.
  4. A rejection happens before the payer decides the claim, so staff correct and resubmit it. A denial happens after the payer decides, so staff use the payer's corrected-claim, reopening, or appeal process. They go to different queues with different owners.
  5. Each step is a separate approval with its own timing. Claims can be approved while ERA or EFT is not, which means payments arrive on paper or by check. Tracking each step by payer and location shows the gap before go-live.
  6. USCDI has several versions with different data elements, so a version tells you what the product can exchange. Check the product's listing on the Certified Health IT Product List (CHPL) and record the criteria and versions.
  7. The client's privacy officer or compliance lead, with legal counsel, decides. The implementer records the request, routes it, and does not change the setting until the decision is made.
  8. Each endpoint can fail on its own side. If the receiving side has no owner, failures and unmatched items sit unseen, so the integration is not ready even when messages move.
  9. Each form, code list, and template has a named owner, a version, an approval, and a change rule. Limiting who can create values prevents near-duplicates. Regular review retires outdated content.
  10. A named, trained operational owner and backup with access to each queue. Thresholds, retry authority, escalation, reconciliation, and evidence rules must be recorded and handed over.
  11. Each prescriber needs identity proofing, a two-factor credential, and an approved place on the access list, after checking the application's audit report. The prescriber and the clinic complete these steps; the implementer tracks them as dated dependencies.

Spaced review

  1. Every difference between source and loaded counts must be explained by population, such as rejected, skipped, or duplicate records. "Within tolerance" is not an explanation. The data owner accepts only after the reconciliation is complete (Module 8).
  2. Do not delete them. The clinical authority sets the rule for uncoded entries, such as pre-visit review and reconciliation at the first visit. You flag and track them under that rule (Module 8).
  3. Closing the loop on results includes failures and unmatched results. The scenario should name who watches that queue. Clinical leadership or the practice manager names the owner; you do not (Module 6).
  4. Attendance is not competence. The approved evaluator must watch her complete a normal and an exception scenario on her own and record the result (Modules 5 and 6).
  5. Write the provider (the prescriber and the credential company), the receiver (the clinic and the project), the need date, the effect of delay (no controlled-substance e-prescribing at go-live), and the escalation path (Module 2).
  6. It is a remembered commitment. Record it as "to be confirmed" with the salesperson as owner and a due date. It is not in scope until someone with authority confirms it (Module 1).
