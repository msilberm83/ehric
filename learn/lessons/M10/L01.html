Module 10, Lesson 1: Start with the work, not the tool

Start with the work, not the tool

A request such as "add AI to intake" cannot be built, tested, or stopped. If you start with the tool, nobody knows what "working" means.

Automation means the system takes a set action when set conditions occur. Artificial intelligence (AI) means software that can sort, predict, summarize, write, pull out facts, or suggest. Its results can vary. Both can remove repeated work. Both can also repeat one error across every record.

So start every proposed use with a use-case statement. It is one short paragraph that says what the use does and who controls it. Fill in this pattern:

For [role and workflow], when [trigger] occurs, the system will [action] using [information]. It produces [result]. [Person or system] reviews, approves, acts on, or monitors it under [rule].

Here are two complete statements:

  • Example A (rules-based). When an approved appointment is one day away, the system sends the set reminder and records delivery. The front-desk lead checks failed deliveries each morning.
  • Example B (AI). When a scanned document enters the intake queue, an AI service proposes a category. Front-desk staff confirm or correct it before routing.

The two uses are very different. Both statements still name the owner, the data, the action, the result, and the control. A request that lacks any of these goes back to the person who asked for it.

Rule: Do not build a use until its statement names the owner, data, action, result, and control.

Check yourself

  1. What five things must every use-case statement name?
  2. A provider emails you: "Let's use AI for referrals." What would you do, and why?
  3. Why is "add AI to intake" not something you can test?

Improve the workflow before automating it

Automation makes a process faster. If the process is unclear, it makes unclear work faster and harder to see.

Use the approved future-state map from Module 4. This is the agreed drawing of how the work will run in the new system. For each proposed use, confirm:

  • the trigger is reliable;
  • the information is available and means what people think it means;
  • responsibility and authority are settled;
  • normal and exception paths are defined;
  • the manual process can be measured today;
  • the use removes useful work, not a control;
  • a failure can be found and fixed;
  • the clinic can stop or bypass the use safely.

If you have worked a front desk, you have seen this. A reminder sent for an appointment that was already moved creates calls, not savings.

Compare two cases. At one clinic, refill requests already go to one queue with one owner, so a reminder rule can help at once. At another clinic, nobody agrees who owns refill requests. A reminder there would send alerts to no one. In both cases the workflow decides whether automation helps.

Rule: Automate only a workflow that is approved, measurable, and safe to stop.

Check yourself

  1. List four things you confirm about a workflow before you automate it.
  2. A clinic wants an automatic task for every unsigned note, but nobody owns unsigned notes. What would you do first?
  3. Why can automation make a weak process worse?

Separate rules-based automation from AI

Rules and AI fail in different ways, so they need different tests. If you test them the same way, you will miss errors in one of them.

Rules-based automation follows set logic: when X happens and conditions A and B are true, do Y. It is deterministic. That means the same input always gives the same output. It can still fail because of bad data, wrong logic, changed workflows, timing, permissions, or a service that is down. Still, you can say exactly what it should do.

Common rules-based uses are:

  • reminders;
  • work-queue routing;
  • raising overdue tasks to a lead;
  • moving documents;
  • approved status changes;
  • alerts about rejected transactions;
  • standard follow-up tasks.

AI-enabled features can give different results for similar input. The result depends on the model, the input, the setup, and changes over time. AI can sound sure of itself and still be wrong or incomplete. If vendor, account, and data controls are weak, it may also expose or keep information in ways the clinic did not plan.

Common AI uses are:

  • drafting visit notes from conversation (ambient notes);
  • summarizing or sorting intake;
  • proposing scheduling replies;
  • analyzing billing denials and drafting letters;
  • sorting documents;
  • drafting letters or text;
  • searching or answering questions over clinic content.

Do not use one review rule for every use. Base it on risk, authority, and clinic policy. Decisions about care, money, access, eligibility, or the official record need a qualified person. Low-risk office suggestions may use sampling or exception review if the approved risk review supports it.

Rule: Test a rule against its exact expected result, and test AI against thresholds set in advance.

Compare two cases. At Cedar Point, a rule should create one nurse task when a refill request stays open for 2 days. You test it against that exact result, and any other result fails. At a pediatric clinic, an AI service sorts scanned documents. Before the final run, the owner sets a threshold, such as 95% correct routing, and you measure against it. In both cases, the passing standard is written down before you test.

Check yourself

  1. What does "deterministic" mean?
  2. Name two ways a rules-based automation can still fail.
  3. Why would a pass rate of "looks right to the reviewer" fail as a test for an AI feature?

Build the use-case inventory

The use-case inventory is the clinic's list of every automation and AI use it turns on. Without it, the clinic cannot see or govern what the system does on its own.

For each use, record:

  • owner and users;
  • purpose and success measure;
  • workflow and trigger;
  • system or vendor;
  • model or feature version, when the vendor provides it;
  • input and output;
  • information type (for example, patient data or payment data);
  • people and decisions affected;
  • possible harms and ways it could fail;
  • reviewer or approval point;
  • status of vendor, contract, privacy, security, and compliance reviews;
  • decision-support source information, when the vendor supplies the decision support (see "Collect decision-support source information");
  • test set and threshold;
  • monitoring and incident owner;
  • fallback and stop method;
  • change and retirement plan.

Product settings alone are not enough. A setting shows that a feature is on. The inventory shows why it is on, who owns it, and how to stop it.

Rule: If a use is not in the inventory, the clinic cannot govern it.

Compare two cases. At Cedar Point, the practice manager asks why reminders went to patients who had cancelled. The reminder rule has no inventory row, so nobody knows who owns it or how to stop it. At a dermatology clinic, staff use an AI tool to draft letters to patients. No row records its vendor, reviewer, or review status. In both cases, the clinic could not govern a use it had not listed.

Check yourself

  1. Name four fields from the inventory that relate to control or stopping.
  2. Why is a list of product settings not a substitute for the inventory?
  3. A vendor turns on a new AI feature by default after an update. What would you do with the inventory?

Run the risk and authority review

A use can harm a patient, cost money, or expose data even when it works as designed. The review makes sure the right owner decides with the risks in view.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a free guide from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). It and its Generative AI Profile describe how to govern, map, measure, and manage AI risk. Use them as general references, and adapt the review to the clinic. Ask questions in five groups.

Purpose and effect. What decision or work could the output change? Could a person be denied, delayed, sent to the wrong place, charged wrongly, exposed, or harmed? Could the output enter the clinical, billing, or official record?

Information. What information goes into the tool? Is it protected health information (PHI), meaning health information that identifies a patient? Is it personal or payment information? May the vendor receive it, keep it, use it to improve its service, or pass it to other companies? Can the clinic get it back, correct it, delete it, or export it?

Authority. Who may approve the use? Who is qualified to check the output? When must a person act before the system goes on? What notices or consent does the clinic require?

Performance. Which errors matter most? Do some groups, languages, document types, or specialties get worse results? Can the output point to approved sources? Can the system show a useful source, confidence level, or reason?

Operations. How will a user know AI was used? How are corrections recorded? How is the version tracked? What happens when the service is down or changes? How are incidents reported, and how is the feature turned off?

Then record the decision and who made it. A feature being available is never a reason to approve it.

Rule: The accountable owner approves each use, and you never approve a high-risk use because the feature exists.

Compare two cases. At Cedar Point, the AI note tool comes with the license, and the practice manager calls it approved. You record that the clinical lead has not reviewed it, and you route the decision there. At a family practice, an AI tool that drafts denial letters is ready to turn on. The billing manager reviews the risks and records the decision. In both cases, an accountable owner decides, not the feature's availability.

Check yourself

  1. Name the five groups of review questions.
  2. Why does the review ask whether some groups or languages get worse results?
  3. The vendor says a feature is "ready to go." Who decides whether the clinic uses it, and what do you record?
