Module 10 — Answers: Check yourself, examples, knowledge check, spaced review

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Start with the work, not the tool. 1. The owner, the data, the action, the result, and the control. 2. Return the request to the provider and ask them to complete a use-case statement. You cannot plan or test a use until it names who owns it, what data it uses, what it does, what it produces, and who controls it. 3. It names no trigger, action, result, or reviewer, so there is no expected behavior to test against.

Improve the workflow before automating it. 1. Any four: the trigger is reliable; the information is available and meaningful; responsibility is settled; normal and exception paths are defined; the manual process can be measured; the use removes work, not a control; failures can be found and fixed; the clinic can stop it safely. 2. Ask the client to settle who owns unsigned notes, and get the workflow approved before you build the task. A task with no owner sends work to no one. 3. Automation speeds up whatever process exists, including its gaps, and makes errors harder to see.

Separate rules-based automation from AI. 1. The same input always gives the same output. 2. Any two: bad data, wrong logic, changed workflow, timing, permissions, or an unavailable service. 3. It has no expected result or threshold set in advance, so the reviewer can pass anything. AI output varies, so it needs a representative set and thresholds fixed before the final run.

Build the use-case inventory. 1. Any four: reviewer or approval point; monitoring and incident owner; fallback and stop method; change and retirement plan; test set and threshold. 2. A setting shows only that a feature is on. It does not show the purpose, owner, risks, tests, or how to stop it. 3. Add the feature to the inventory, mark it as not yet approved, and ask the owner whether it should stay on until the review is done.

Run the risk and authority review. 1. Purpose and effect, information, authority, performance, and operations. 2. A tool can work well on average and badly for one group, which could cause harm the average hides. 3. The accountable owner decides. You record the decision, who made it, and the date.

Review the vendor and the deployment. 1. Any four: data use and retention; training on clinic data; other companies that handle the data; security; logging; incident notice; availability; model changes; export and contract end; required agreements. 2. A company that handles PHI for the clinic. 3. Hold the feature and send the data terms to the privacy, security, and contract owners. Appointment data identifies patients, so it is PHI.

Collect decision-support source information. 1. An evidence-based DSI follows published research or guidelines. A predictive DSI learns patterns from training data and produces a prediction. 2. Any four: intended use; intended patients and users; out-of-scope uses and known risks; training data and inputs; fairness approach; outside testing; performance results; ongoing checks in local data. 3. Copy the source attributes into the inventory, flag the hospital-to-clinic mismatch in writing, and take it to the decision-support owner. Propose a local test before routine use.

Design human oversight. 1. Any four: authority, knowledge and context, time, access to information, a clear standard, the power to reject or stop, and a record. 2. They cannot study the output or reject it, so the "review" changes nothing. 3. A weekly sample review, because errors are low-risk and staff confirm each category during routing anyway.

Build a representative evaluation. 1. If thresholds are set after the results, they can be moved to make a weak result pass. 2. Any five: version; setup and prompt version; connected sources; test-set version; date and environment; evaluator and answer; output; result and error type; decision and threshold. 3. Report the Spanish-language result separately as below threshold. The overall pass does not cover it.

Test rules-based automation carefully. 1. Any five from the list: fires once; does not fire without conditions; no duplicate work; timing and time zone; role and access; right queue; cancel or change; downstream confirmation; outage; retry; audit; stop. 2. A correction can start the same chain of messages and tasks again. 3. Log a defect with the failed step, then retest the fix and every action the trigger starts.

Apply the domain controls. 1. Any four: missing facts, wrong speaker, negation, medication or measurement errors, unrelated content, carried-forward text, unsupported statements. 2. The clinician has not yet confirmed the content, so a claim could be built on wrong facts. 3. The privacy officer and the clinical lead.

Monitor after release. 1. Any four: volume, quality and error, correction and override, group checks, privacy and incident signals, speed and availability. 2. It may mean staff stopped checking, not that the tool improved. 3. Ask for the change details, rerun the approved evaluation set, and compare results with the thresholds before the new version reaches users.

Check the plan against the 2025 SAFER Guides. 1. Practice 1.9. 2. AI problems can happen at night or on weekends. If nobody can stop the tool then, harm continues. 3. Explain that the client's governance owner does the self-assessment. Offer the completed crosswalk as evidence.

Worked example prompts

Prompt 1. The evaluation results decide whether the pilot may start. Building the set first keeps the start date from pushing the team to skip or rush testing.

Prompt 2. Limiting where a clinical tool is used is a clinical decision. The specialist brings the evidence; the clinical lead owns patient safety for documentation.

Prompt 3. The problem appears in one visit type. An overall rate would hide it, so the measure must track that type on its own.

Faded example

  1. Produced: a risk and authority decision record signed by the office manager and the clinical lead. Why: a wrong category can send a lab report to the school-form queue, where nobody reviews results, so the clinical lead must approve how lab reports are handled.
  2. Produced: a vendor and data decision record. You find out whether the vendor keeps the documents, uses them to train models, or passes them to other companies. The privacy, security, and contract owners decide. Why: scanned documents contain PHI.
  3. Produced: a review pattern. Staff confirm every category before routing, and the office manager samples corrections monthly. Staff need time per document and a "correct" option, not just "accept." Why: a reviewer without time or the power to correct is not a control.
  4. Produced: a test set with thresholds set in advance, broken out by category. It includes each document type, poor scans, handwritten forms, documents for the wrong patient, mixed documents, and an outage. Why: an average can hide a dangerous error, such as lab reports sorted as "other."
  5. Produced: a monitoring plan with an owner, measures for corrections by category and review time, a threshold to pause, and written stop steps for the clinic's on-call contact, tested before go-live. Why: performance can drift, and someone must be able to stop the tool at any hour.

Knowledge check

  1. It must name the role and workflow, the trigger, the action, the information used, the result, and who reviews or controls it under what rule. In short: owner, data, action, result, and control. Without these, the use cannot be built, tested, or stopped.
  2. Rules-based automation is deterministic, so you test it against an exact expected result for each trigger and condition. AI output can vary with the model, input, and setup. It needs a representative evaluation set with thresholds set in advance and results by category.
  3. The reviewer needs the authority to decide, the knowledge and context to spot errors, time to review, access to supporting information, a clear standard, and the power to reject, correct, or stop. The review must be recorded where required. A habitual "approve" click is not oversight.
  4. Thresholds set after the results can be moved to fit them, so the test proves nothing. Setting them first, with a date, keeps the release decision honest and traceable.
  5. Record the system, model, and feature version; setup and prompt version; connected sources; test-set version; date and environment; evaluator and approved answer; output; result and error type; and the decision and threshold. This lets the team compare behavior after a change and spot drift.
  6. The monitoring plan sets thresholds for investigation, pause, rollback, or retirement. Typical triggers are error or correction rates past a threshold, a group falling below its threshold, a privacy or security incident, service problems, or an unreviewed model or vendor change.
  7. Source attributes for a predictive DSI include intended use, patients, and users; out-of-scope uses and known risks; training data and inputs; fairness approach; outside testing and performance; and ongoing checks, including in local data. The client's decision-support or AI governance owner decides whether, where, and for whom to turn it on.
  8. The item on clear written steps and authority for IT staff to turn off AI 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. A stop only the vendor's weekday desk can perform does not meet it. Name a client on-call role, write the steps, and test them.

Spaced review

  1. Record transport, destination, and business results separately. Transport passed, but the business result failed because the lab could not act on the order. Log a defect against the business result and retest end to end.
  2. A rejection happens before the payer decides; front-end edits stopped the claim, and staff fix and resubmit it. A denial happens after the payer decides and appears on the 835; it is worked through corrected claims or appeals. They go to different queues with different owners.
  3. Add peer review of the tests and name someone other than the builder to make the release decision. This gives the independence QC needs.
  4. Record it as a decision for the clinical owner of the protocol and route it through the controlled path from Module 5. You teach routing; clinical leadership owns the protocol and decides any change.
  5. It is a remembered commitment, not scope. Record it in the commitments table and decision log with an owner and a due date to verify it with someone who has authority.
