Module 10, Lesson 2: Review the vendor and the deployment

Review the vendor and the deployment

Many AI features send clinic data to the vendor or to other companies. If no one checks the terms, patient data may be used in ways the clinic never agreed to.

Confirm that the clinic's approved process covers:

  • contract and service terms;
  • how data is used, kept, deleted, and used to train models;
  • other companies that handle the data, and where they are;
  • security and access controls;
  • logging and audit;
  • incident notice;
  • service availability and recovery;
  • changes to the model or feature;
  • how the clinic's data is kept apart from other customers' data;
  • export and contract end;
  • any required agreements, such as a business associate agreement.

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) sets privacy and security rules for PHI. For uses that involve PHI, bring in the client's privacy, security, legal, and contract owners. A business associate is a company that handles PHI for the clinic. Current guidance from the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) says a cloud provider that stores electronic PHI for a clinic may be a business associate. This holds even when the data is encrypted and the provider has no key.

Compare two cases. A scheduling assistant may look like "only scheduling," but appointment data still identifies patients and their care. A spelling checker that runs only on the user's computer may send no data out at all. In both cases, the data flow, not the feature name, decides who must review.

Rule: If a use sends PHI outside the clinic, the client's privacy, security, and contract owners decide before it is turned on.

Check yourself

  1. Name four vendor terms the clinic must review.
  2. What is a business associate?
  3. A manager says, "Just turn it on; it's only scheduling." What would you do, and why?

Collect decision-support source information

Clinicians may act on a decision-support alert or score without knowing what it was built on. If nobody collects the developer's facts, the clinic cannot judge whether the tool fits its own patients.

Many electronic health record (EHR) systems now include decision support, and some of it is AI. A federal rule tells the developer what it must disclose. Your job is to ask for those facts and record them. You collect them and pass them along. You do not judge them.

What the rule covers

The Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology (ONC) runs the federal Health IT (health information technology) Certification Program. For a time it was called ASTP/ONC, after the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy (ASTP). A notice published April 1, 2026, returned it to the name ONC.

ONC's HTI-1 final rule was published January 9, 2024. It replaced the old clinical decision support criterion with a decision support interventions (DSI) criterion, 45 CFR 170.315(b)(11). (CFR is the Code of Federal Regulations.) A DSI is technology that supports clinical decisions, such as an alert, a reminder, or a risk score. The rule names two kinds:

  • Evidence-based DSIs follow published research or guidelines. Example: a reminder that a patient with diabetes is due for an eye exam.
  • Predictive DSIs learn patterns from training data. They then produce a prediction, a category, or a recommendation. Example: a model that scores a patient's risk of a hospital admission.

Certified developers had to meet the new criterion by December 31, 2024. From January 1, 2025, only the DSI criterion counts toward the certified "Base EHR."

Source attributes

Source attributes are the facts a developer must make available about each DSI it supplies. For evidence-based DSIs, they include:

  • the citation, the developer, and the funding source;
  • release dates;
  • whether the DSI uses race, ethnicity, language, sex, date of birth, or social factors such as housing.

For predictive DSIs, the list is longer. It includes:

  • intended use, patients, and users;
  • uses that are out of scope, and known risks;
  • training data and inputs;
  • how fairness was addressed;
  • outside testing and performance results;
  • how accuracy and fairness are checked over time, including in local data (the client's own patients).

If some facts are not available, the product must say so. The certified software must let a small set of named client users view these facts in plain language.

The rule covers DSIs the developer supplies in certified software. It does not cover a tool the clinic builds itself or a separate product that is not certified.

What you do

  1. List every vendor-supplied DSI the client plans to turn on. Mark which ones are predictive.
  2. Ask the vendor where the source attributes appear and which client role can view them.
  3. Copy the key facts into the inventory: intended patients and setting, out-of-scope uses, local testing status, and any "not available" fields.
  4. Flag mismatches. A model built for hospital inpatients may not fit an outpatient clinic.
  5. Take the record to the client's decision-support or AI governance owner. That owner decides whether, where, and for whom to turn on the DSI.

You do not judge a model clinically fit. You do not turn on a predictive DSI because the software is certified. Certification means the facts are disclosed. It does not mean the model suits this clinic.

Rule status

In December 2025, ONC proposed the HTI-5 rule. Among other changes, it would narrow the DSI criterion and remove the predictive "model card" source-attribute requirements. On September 30, 2026, HTI-5 was still a proposal, and the current regulation still lists the source attributes. Check the rule's status at each project start. If the requirement is removed, keep asking for the same facts as good practice.

Rule: Certification means the facts are disclosed, not that the model fits this clinic.

Compare two cases. At Cedar Point, a certified predictive DSI lists local testing as "not available." You copy that gap into the inventory and flag it for the decision-support owner. At a pediatric clinic, a certified risk score lists its intended patients as adults only. You flag the mismatch, because the clinic treats children. In both cases, certification made the facts visible, and the owner still decides whether the tool fits.

Design human oversight

"A person reviews it" is a weak control if that person lacks time, knowledge, or the power to say no. Real oversight is designed, not assumed.

Human in the loop means a person checks or approves AI output before it has an effect. It works only when the person has:

  • authority to make the decision;
  • enough knowledge and context to spot an error;
  • time to review;
  • access to the supporting information;
  • a clear standard;
  • the power to reject, correct, stop, or escalate;
  • a record of the review when one is required.

A person who clicks "approve" out of habit is not a control.

Choose a review pattern for each use:

  • review every output before action;
  • review outputs above a set risk or confidence level;
  • review exceptions only;
  • review a sample of routine low-risk outputs;
  • watch totals and trends only;
  • forbid the AI from certain actions.

Compare two uses. A portal reply that answers a question about symptoms needs a clinician to review every draft. A suggested document category for a routine form may need only a weekly sample. Both patterns match the reviewer's authority and time to the risk.

Base the pattern on risk and clinic policy. Review it again when the use, data, model, workflow, or failure history changes.

Rule: A reviewer counts as a control only with the authority, knowledge, time, and power to reject.

Check yourself

  1. List four things a reviewer needs for oversight to work.
  2. Why is a reviewer with 30 seconds and one "approve" button not a real control?
  3. Which review pattern would you suggest for a low-risk document category suggestion, and why?

Build a representative evaluation

A demonstration shows the tool at its best. Your test must show how the tool performs on this clinic's real mix of work.

Test the workflow as the clinic set it up, not a vendor demonstration. An evaluation set is the approved group of test cases you run through the tool. It should include:

  • common cases;
  • incomplete, unclear, and poor-quality input;
  • important groups, languages, document types, or specialties;
  • requests the tool should refuse;
  • misleading content, where it applies;
  • limits on protected information;
  • failures that would cause serious harm;
  • service outage and fallback;
  • attempts to override instructions or expose restricted information;
  • changes in workflow or model behavior.

A threshold is the result a use must meet to pass. Set expected results and thresholds before you see the final results. Measures may include:

  • accuracy, or agreement with approved answers;
  • false positives and false negatives;
  • completeness;
  • made-up or unsupported statements;
  • how often staff correct the output, and how long it takes;
  • routing accuracy;
  • forbidden disclosure;
  • how often users override the output;
  • speed and availability;
  • the effect on the work.

An average can hide a dangerous group or error type. Record results by category.

Keep a traceable record

For each run, record:

  • system, model, and feature version, when available;
  • setup and instruction (prompt) version;
  • connected sources;
  • test-set version;
  • date and environment;
  • evaluator and approved answer;
  • output;
  • result and error type;
  • decision and threshold.

AI services change. You may not be able to repeat every output exactly. You can still keep enough detail to compare behavior and spot drift, which is a slow change in performance over time.

Rule: Set thresholds before the final run, and report results by category.

Compare two cases. At Cedar Point, an AI document sorter scores 96% overall, but poor-quality faxed referrals score 78%. You report both numbers, so the owner sees the weak category. At a family practice, the team sees the final results and asks to lower the threshold from 95% to 90%. You report the result against the threshold set before the run. In both cases, the record compares real results with a standard set in advance.
