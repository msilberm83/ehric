Module 5 — Answers: Check yourself, examples, knowledge check, spaced review

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Tie training to the approved build. (1) Purchased outcome, workflow requirement, configuration item, training objective, test case, approval. (2) Users reach go-live without knowing how to do work the clinic approved, so the work fails or staff invent a workaround. (3) Leave it out of the session. If the provider wants the feature, log it as a new requirement or scope question for its owner.

Plan by role. (1) Any four of: tasks, allowed decisions, information they may access, prerequisites, normal and exception scenarios, competence evidence, recheck process. (2) Work is lost at handoffs. The sender must know what the receiver needs, and the receiver must know how to return incomplete work. (3) Do not teach the workaround. The client approved the access model. If the supervisor thinks the role needs the permission, log it as a decision for the access owner.

Train super users first. (1) The clinic chooses. You record each name, role, location, shift, and protected hours. (2) They must be competent before they can help in later sessions and support peers at go-live. (3) Explain that super users need protected time and backfill to learn and support others. Ask the practice manager to decide on cover, and record the decision and any risk.

Plan for every learner. (1) The client's HR or accommodation owner decides. You record the accommodation, not the reason or diagnosis. (2) Automatic captions often garble drug and product names, so learners who rely on them get wrong information. (3) Use one name for the screen everywhere, add numbered text steps, and add a short glossary.

Prepare the training environment. (1) The exact version of the build being taught. (2) You cannot tell which build the learner proved competence on, so the record may not match what goes live. (3) Run the session as a labeled demonstration and hold the competence checks until acceptance is recorded.

Time final training close to go-live. (1) Validation sessions are early, with key users, to find design problems. Final training is close to go-live, on the tested release, and produces competence evidence. (2) Screens can change between releases. The record must show the learner proved competence on the release that goes live. (3) Explain the risk of forgetting and of later changes. Recommend validation sessions now and final training in the last weeks, with refreshers for changes. The clinic decides; record the decision.

Compensate when there is no separate training environment. (1) Any four: obvious test-patient names, limited creation rights, outbound messages off, reports exclude test patients, on-screen environment label, cleanup checks, a record of differences. (2) Final training often comes after conversion has loaded real patient data, so the copy is no longer pre-live. (3) The client's accountable authority must choose the option, after hearing from privacy and security, and you must record that decision.

Write observable learning objectives. (1) For example: "Cancel an appointment with the approved reason and send the cancellation notice." (2) It names an action you can watch, so you can judge pass or recheck.

Run the training loop. (1) Show, ask, set up through the controlled path, practice, teach back and recover. (2) The workflow is already approved. Class time confirms it and surfaces gaps; it does not reopen design. (3) A required field affects data and tested behavior, so it is not a low-risk item. Log it and route it to the decision and change process.

Teach the general workflow families. (1) Inquiry and first contact, scheduling, intake, forms and signatures, queues and work status. (2) Real work is not always clean. Staff must recover from duplicates, missing data, and failures without inventing a process. (3) Rewrite it so schedulers route the caller by the approved script. Clinical urgency is a clinical decision.

Record competence, not attendance. (1) Release ID, scenario and exception, result, and observer with date. (2) Readiness belongs to each learner. Others in the group may not be able to do the work.

Route open items and request QC. (1) Training question, coaching need, defect, new requirement, policy decision, homework. (2) The learner did the work right, so the build is wrong. Practice cannot fix it; a tested fix can. (3) Name each owner, step, date, and check, for example: "Practice manager sends approved forms by May 6; builder loads by May 8; QC tests by May 10."

Worked example prompts

  1. Super users must be competent before they can co-lead practice and answer peers. Training them first also tests the session plan before the larger groups.
  2. The learner completed the form correctly, so the fault is in the build. Only a tested fix through QC removes it.
  3. Their earlier result was on the faulty routing. Competence must match the corrected release that will go live.

Faded example

  1. List the options still open (test environment shared in time, if one exists, or production with test patients) and the controls for each: obvious test names, limited creation rights, outbound messages off, reports exclude test patients, on-screen label, cleanup checks. The clinic's accountable authority decides after hearing privacy and security. You produce a written environment decision.
  2. Train the super user first, on the go-live release, then the medical assistants in the last two to three weeks. You produce a training schedule and competence records. The super user can then help in class and on go-live day.
  3. A real message from a test patient is a defect in the controls, with possible privacy impact. Stop the exercise, log a high-priority defect, tell the privacy lead, and send it to QC before training continues.
  4. Release ID, scenario and exception, result, observer, and date for each learner, plus rechecks for anyone not yet competent.

Knowledge check

  1. Staff need to do their own work, not learn every menu. Role-based training covers the tasks, decisions, and access each role has, including handoffs to other roles. It also avoids teaching screens a role cannot use.
  2. Attendance shows someone was in the room. Competence shows the person completed a normal scenario and an exception alone while someone watched. Only competence shows readiness for go-live.
  3. Only items on the approved low-risk list are set up in class, and each is recorded and sent to QC. Anything that affects access, clinical work, data, billing, integrations, safety, or tested behavior goes through decision, change, build, and QC.
  4. Approved scenarios reflect real work the client agreed to. They keep training tied to the traceability record, so learners practice what will actually happen after go-live.
  5. The client's qualified owners approve controlled form content, such as consent or privacy wording. Accountable clinical, privacy, or billing leaders approve professional workflow decisions. The implementer loads approved content and checks its behavior.
  6. The learner, alone, recognizes the exception (such as a duplicate or failed signature), takes the approved recovery steps, and explains why. A teach-back adds evidence that the learner understands rather than memorizes.
  7. Competence is only meaningful for a known build. Recording the environment and release shows learners proved competence on what will go live, and shows who needs a refresher if it changes.
  8. The client's accountable authority decides, after hearing from privacy and security. You document the options, the risks, and controls such as test-patient naming, outbound messages off, report exclusion, on-screen labels, and cleanup checks.
  9. The client's HR or accommodation owner decides. You record the accommodation provided, not the reason, and make sure your materials can deliver it.
  10. People forget steps they do not use, and screens can change after QC, conversion, or interface work. Final training close to go-live, on the tested release, gives competence records that match what goes live.
  11. The clinic chooses super users, ideally people peers trust, with protected time. They are trained first so they can help in later sessions and support peers at go-live.

Spaced review

  1. Record where the requirement comes from and who confirms it (Module 4, separate policy from habit). Do not build it as law, and do not drop it, until the source is confirmed.
  2. It is an optional improvement, or a future phase if deferred with an owner and review date. It does not feed the go-live plan (Module 4).
  3. A target date can move through normal planning. Plan backward from the new date to check whether review, training, and correction time still fit, and raise any conflict with the sponsor (Module 2).
  4. "Unlimited training" is remembered, not documented. Log it in the commitments table with an owner and due date to confirm with sales and the client (Module 1).
  5. The record must go out within one business day. Without it, decisions, owners, and dates are not confirmed in writing, and people work from memory (Module 3).
