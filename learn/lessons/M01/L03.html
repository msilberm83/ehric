Module 1, Lesson 3: Turn each purchased item into a named resource

Turn each purchased item into a named resource

Work without a named owner waits until it becomes urgent. By then, it may already delay go-live.

Build the project team from the work. Start with each purchased item and ask which expertise it needs. An interface is a connection that moves data between two systems. A clearinghouse is a company that checks insurance claims and passes them to many health plans.

Purchased item or condition Likely resource First question
Standard software deployment Deployment or platform administrator What environment, access, and date are required?
Custom feature, report, or service Product and development Is it approved, feasible, estimated, and scheduled?
Scanner, signature pad, card reader, or workstation work Technical or device support Is the device supported in the target environment?
Data conversion Data conversion lead and client data owner What sources, fields, volumes, quality, and retention rules apply?
Clearinghouse, payment, laboratory, e-signature, or other interface Integration lead plus business owner Who owns each endpoint, contract, credential, and test?
Clinical workflow Clinical leader and patient-safety reviewer Who approves the workflow and checks the build?
Protected or confidential information Privacy, security, and compliance What access, contract, risk, and audit requirements apply?
Training across multiple roles Training or change lead plus department managers Who must show they can do the work before go-live?
Any configured module Quality control What test evidence is required before acceptance?

Small projects may have one person in several roles. Record each role anyway.

Rule: Every purchased item leads to a named person and one accountable decision-maker.

Example A. "Alex" is not a control. "Alex, accountable for client data validation" is.

Example B. For signature pads, "device team" is not enough. "Priya, device support, confirms the model is supported by October 10" is.

Both entries tie a named person to a specific duty that someone can check.

Check yourself

  1. Which roles does data conversion create, and who approves the converted data?
  2. On a small project, one person fills three roles. Why record each role separately?

Open the control logs and file each entry correctly

Risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies need different actions. A wrong label sends a problem to the wrong response.

Start the project's control logs now, in the management system. That is the shared project record where tasks, owners, dates, and decisions live.

Risk. A future event that might affect the project. Record how likely it is, its impact, owner, response, trigger, and review date. Example: The source vendor may not deliver the export by the planned test-conversion date.

Assumption. Something the plan treats as true but nobody has checked. Record how and when it will be confirmed. Example: The plan assumes the new system supports the existing scanners.

Issue. A problem that exists now and needs resolution. Example: The signed order lists two locations, while the proposal and a client email list three.

Dependency. Work that cannot finish until another person, team, vendor, approval, or event acts. Example: Interface testing depends on the clearinghouse issuing test credentials.

To tell a risk from an issue, ask one question: has it already happened? A conflict between two sources, a missing document, or a number that does not match is true today. Log it as an issue, even if its effects come later. A risk may or may not happen.

Rule: If it has already happened, log it as an issue, even if its effects come later.

Example A. "The order lists 14 provider licenses, but the sponsor's email lists 17 providers." The mismatch exists today. It is an issue.

Example B. "The practice may hire two more providers before go-live." It may never happen. It is a risk.

Both are about provider counts. What separates them is whether the problem already exists.

Keep a decision log next to the other logs. Record the date, question, options, decision, decision-maker, reason, affected work, and evidence. Later, someone will ask why something was set up a certain way. The decision log should answer without relying on anyone's memory.

Check yourself

  1. Name the four log types, and the question that separates an issue from a risk.
  2. "The clinic believes its label printers will work with the new system." Which log type is this, and what do you record with it?
  3. Why does the decision log matter months after the decision?

Worked example: Cedar Point handoff

Cedar Point Internal Medicine has two locations: Cedar Point main and Lakeshore. It buys an EHR with practice-management functions (scheduling, registration, and billing). It also buys electronic forms, a clearinghouse connection, payment processing, and twenty signature pads. The order covers conversion of patient demographics and upcoming appointments, plus remote go-live support. The salesperson remembers telling the practice administrator that a lab results interface was included. The signed order and its attached proposal do not list one.

  1. Collected the record before the call. The implementer asked sales for the signed order, attached proposal, SOW, and sales notes. Produced: a document list. Why: reading the order early showed that no lab interface appears in the signed documents.
  2. Ran the six-part handoff. The implementer asked what result made the purchase worth it. Produced: a handoff record with the outcome "stop entering intake twice, and have both locations live by January." Why: a specific outcome can be planned and tested.
  3. Sorted the commitments. The purchased modules, devices, conversion, training, and support were documented. The lab interface was remembered. Produced: a commitments table. The lab interface is to be confirmed, owned by the salesperson, due before kickoff. Why: the interface is neither silently added nor silently dropped.
  4. Wrote the scope summary. Produced: in scope, out of scope, and to be confirmed lists, with the lab interface in the last group. Why: the team can plan the documented work now and see the open item.
  1. Stated how they would know it works. Produced: acceptance evidence, such as "a patient completes intake online and the answers file to the correct chart." Also a success measure: "front-desk minutes per new-patient check-in," owned by the practice administrator. Why: the clinic's reason for buying now has a result someone can count.
  2. Built the resource map. Produced: named roles for clinical leadership, billing, privacy and security, data, deployment, devices, interfaces, training, and quality control (QC), each with one accountable person. Why: every purchased item has someone who will act on it.
  3. Opened the control logs. Produced: the clearinghouse contract and the signature-pad delivery as dependencies. The January date went in as a risk until staffing, data, interfaces, and clinic calendars are reviewed. Why: the plan shows what could move the date before anyone treats it as fixed.
  4. Checked the gate. Produced: a dated client introduction, assigned to the salesperson. Why: the project cannot start planning until the client knows who you are and what comes next.

Why do you think the implementer listed the lab interface as to be confirmed rather than out of scope?

Why was the January date logged as a risk and not as an issue?

Why did the implementer agree on a success measure before any build work began?

That record gives Module 2 enough to build a first roadmap without presenting guesses as commitments.

A one-location pediatric clinic buys an EHR with a patient portal. The order also includes two custom vaccine-consent forms, conversion of immunization histories, and an interface to the state immunization registry. The sales notes say the representative "promised Spanish-language portal screens." The signed order lists the portal but no language options. The clinic wants to go live before flu-vaccine season.

  1. Collected the record. Done. The order and SOW arrived. The proposal is referred to but not attached. It is logged as a missing document, owned by the sales representative.
  2. Ran the handoff. Done. Outcome recorded: "Parents complete consent forms online before the visit, and immunization histories stay complete."
  3. Sorted the commitments. Done. The portal (order) and the consent forms (SOW) are documented. The Spanish screens are remembered: to be confirmed, owned by the sales representative, due before kickoff.

Field assignment

Use the Cedar Point Internal Medicine practice case from the worked example. Complete Appendix A and create these records:

  1. Handoff record: who, why, purchased items, documented commitments, remembered commitments, constraints, and next actions.
  2. Initial scope: in scope, out of scope, to be confirmed, early acceptance evidence, and a success measure and owner for each purchased outcome.
  3. Client profile: organization, locations, user groups, current system, target system, desired outcomes, and relevant review areas.
  4. Resource map: work item, responsible role, accountable decision-maker, first required action, and needed date.
  5. Control logs: at least two realistic entries across risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies, plus any decision already made.

Where the case does not give a fact, record it as to be confirmed and name the role that would know. Do not invent approvals or names. Use only the practice case. Never place real patient, client, or employer information in course work, even with names removed.
