Module 1, Lesson 1: Know what you own and what others decide

Know what you own and what others decide

A decision that nobody owns does not get made. The gap shows up later as a delay, a rework, or a safety problem.

The implementation specialist, called the implementer in this book, turns a signed sale into coordinated work that can be tested. You own the connection between tasks. The order leads to scope. Scope leads to resources. Resources lead to a roadmap. The roadmap leads to a system that people use.

Owning the connection is different from owning every decision. On the job, the people on the buyer's side who have a role in the EMR are called stakeholders. That includes the buyer, the clinic leaders, the providers and staff who will use the system, and the super users: staff trained early who help their coworkers. You and the rest of the vendor team are not stakeholders; you serve them. Each person below owns a different kind of decision:

  • The salesperson owns the accuracy of the commercial handoff.
  • The client sponsor owns business priorities. The sponsor is the clinic leader who approved the purchase, so teams often just call this person the buyer.
  • Clinical leaders own decisions within their professional authority.
  • Privacy, security, finance, billing, technical, and quality specialists approve work in their areas.

You make sure each decision is assigned, made, recorded, and carried into the build. If you have worked a front desk, you have seen this already: a manager approves a schedule change, and you carry it out.

For every major item, record four roles. Together they form a responsibility chart. On the job, everyone calls it a RACI chart (say it "RAY-see"), from the first letter of each role.

The four role names are standard, so you need to know them as they are. They are easier to remember if you turn each one into a short action in the same form:

Letter Role This person…
R Responsible does the work
A Accountable approves the result
C Consulted is asked before the decision
I Informed is told after the decision

Another way to remember: the first two roles do something (do and approve), and the last two are spoken to (asked before, told after). Each item has only one A.

One person can be both R and A. That is common, especially at a small clinic, where the office manager may do a task and also approve it. Ask for a separate approver when the work is high-risk, for example:

  • the vendor does the work, but the result belongs to the clinic, such as converted patient data;
  • the work affects patient safety, privacy, security, or billing;
  • the contract or the client's policy requires a second approval.

You do not need a chart for every small task. You do need clear accountability wherever a missed decision could affect scope, cost, security, safety, privacy, billing, or the go-live date. Go-live is the day the clinic starts using the new system for real work.

Rule: You own the connection between decisions, not the decisions themselves.

Example A. The medical director decides how abnormal lab results reach providers. You book the review, record the decision, and send it to the build team.

Example B. The billing manager decides which payment plans the front desk may offer. You give the decision a due date, record it, and check that the build matches it.

In both cases, a qualified clinic person decides. Your part is to get the decision assigned, recorded, and built.

Before the sale. Before you arrive, the clinic usually assessed its readiness, set goals, compared products, watched demonstrations, and signed a contract. You do not repeat that work. Ask for its results instead: the clinic's goals and any readiness assessment, the requirements or demonstration scripts used to choose the product, and anything promised during the demonstration. Collect them with the rest of the sales paperwork before the handoff meeting. If the clinic says, "We assumed the system could…," check whether that promise is written in the signed contract. If it is not, treat it as a remembered promise, not a sure one. The section "Sort each commitment" shows how.

Check yourself

  1. Name the four roles you record for each major item.
  2. A clinic leader asks you to decide which staff can see lab results, "since you know the system." What would you do, and why?
  3. Why do you ask for the clinic's buying-stage results if you will not repeat that work?

Collect the commercial record before the handoff

If you see the record for the first time during the call, you cannot spot gaps or conflicts in time. Problems found late cost the clinic weeks.

The sales-to-implementation handoff is the meeting where the sales representative transfers the project to you. A friendly introduction is useful, but the purpose is operational. After the meeting, you should know five things:

  • what was sold;
  • why it was sold;
  • what the client expects;
  • what is still uncertain;
  • who can resolve each uncertainty.

Ask for the commercial record before the call:

  • the signed order (the signed agreement or order form that lists what the clinic bought), the statement of work (SOW), which describes the services to be delivered, and any approved change documents;
  • the proposal and product configuration;
  • sales notes and recorded commitments;
  • locations, user counts, departments, and purchased modules;
  • requested interfaces, data conversion, devices, forms, and custom work;
  • expected dates or contract milestones;
  • the client sponsor, the day-to-day project owner, and known subject-matter experts;
  • known objections, constraints, dependencies, and open questions.

Use approved systems to receive and store these records. Do not ask sales to send production data as background. Production data is real data from a live system, such as patient records. Also refuse passwords and other restricted information.

Rule: Get the signed record before the handoff call, and never accept real patient data as background.

Example A. Sales sends you the order and the SOW two days before the call. You notice the order lists two locations and the proposal lists three. You bring the conflict to the call as an open question.

Example B. Sales offers a spreadsheet of real patient appointments "so you can see their volume." You decline and ask for counts instead.

In both cases, you prepare from approved documents. You find problems early without moving data you should not have.

Check yourself

  1. List five kinds of documents you ask sales for before the handoff.
  2. Sales offers to email you real patient records so you can see the data. What do you do, and why?

Run the handoff meeting in six parts

A meeting without an agenda tends to cover the friendly parts and skip the gaps. The six parts make sure you leave with what planning needs.

Allow 30 to 45 minutes for a standard implementation. Allow more for a complex one. Use this order.

1. Commercial purpose. Ask why the client bought the system. Ask what result was important enough to approve the purchase. Record the client's words when you have them. "Replace the old system" is not yet a useful outcome. "Move intake online, stop entering it twice, and support two new locations by January" gives the team something to plan and test.

2. Purchased scope. Review the signed order line by line. Translate each item into likely work, but do not claim to know the full effort before discovery. The order is the commercial starting specification: it controls what was sold. Discovery will show how the purchased items fit the clinic's workflow, data, and duties. If the sales description conflicts with the signed documents, log the conflict and assign it. Do not quietly expand or reduce scope.

3. People and authority. Identify who signed, the executive sponsor, the day-to-day client project owner, and the operational leaders. Ask who can approve workflow, data, security, billing, and go-live decisions. Ask where disagreement already exists. Do not label a person a "resistor" in records the client will see. Describe the actual concern instead. For example, the billing manager questions the conversion plan, or two clinicians prefer the old note workflow. Specific concerns can be managed. Labels usually make them worse.

4. Commitments and expectations. Ask sales to name every commitment that could affect the work:

  • dates;
  • features or interfaces;
  • conversion scope;
  • custom work;
  • staffing or on-site support;
  • training hours;
  • reports, forms, or templates;
  • pricing that depends on implementation choices.

Separate written commitments from remembered ones. The next section shows how.

5. Known risks and dependencies. Ask what could make the project late, unsafe, or unacceptable. Common examples:

  • an outside contract that is not signed;
  • custom work with no estimate;
  • poor source data or uncertain device support;
  • a lease or construction date;
  • staff turnover or a pending security review;
  • a go-live date chosen without input from clinic operations.

6. Client introduction and next action. Agree who will introduce you to the client and what the introduction will say. The client should know your role, the next step, and what you need from them. End with named actions and dates.

Rule: End the handoff with named actions, owners, and dates.

Example A. "Implementation will follow up" is not an action. "Jordan will send the signed interface addendum by Thursday" is.

Example B. "The billing manager is difficult" cannot be managed. "The billing manager questions how old balances will convert" can be assigned and answered.

Both examples turn a vague statement into something a named person can act on.

Check yourself

  1. Name the six parts of the handoff agenda, in order.
  2. Sales says the billing manager "is a resistor." How do you record this, and why?
  3. Why is "Implementation will follow up" not an acceptable way to end the handoff?
