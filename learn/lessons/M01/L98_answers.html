Module 1 — Answers

Answers for this chapter

Check yourself

Know what you own and what others decide

  1. Responsible (does the work), accountable (approves the result), consulted (gives input before the decision), and informed (needs the result but does not approve it).
  2. Decline to make the decision. Who may see lab results is a privacy and clinical decision for the clinic's authorities. Offer to set up the review, bring the options, record their decision, and carry it into the build.
  3. Those results are inputs. The clinic's goals tell you why it bought. Demonstration scripts and promises show what the clinic expects. Anything it "assumed" the system could do must be sorted as documented or remembered.

Collect the commercial record before the handoff

  1. Any five of: the signed order, the SOW, approved change documents, the proposal and product configuration, sales notes, the list of locations and users, requested interfaces and conversion, expected dates, client contacts, and known open questions.
  2. Decline the records. Production data is not background material, and it should not move by email. Ask for counts or a description instead. Real samples come later through the approved conversion process.

Run the handoff meeting in six parts

  1. Commercial purpose; purchased scope; people and authority; commitments and expectations; known risks and dependencies; client introduction and next action.
  2. Record the specific concern, such as "questions how old balances will convert." A label like "resistor" in a client-facing record damages the relationship and gives nobody a concern to resolve.
  3. It has no named person and no date. Nobody can check whether it happened. A proper close names who does what, by when.

Sort each commitment: documented or remembered

  1. The signed order or agreement, the statement of work, a proposal the order includes or attaches, and an approved change.
  2. Treat it as to be confirmed. A proposal is documented only when the signed order includes it. Ask sales or the contracts team whether it forms part of the sale. Give the question an owner and a due date.
  3. The client may have bought because of it, and it may turn out to be true. Calling it out of scope decides a commercial question you do not own. "To be confirmed" keeps it visible until the right person decides.

Write the initial scope summary

  1. In scope, out of scope, and to be confirmed.
  2. It is purchased, so it belongs in the summary. But it is not defined. List each open question (sources, counts, dates, who validates) as to be confirmed, with the person who can answer it. Hiding the gaps in broad words means nobody plans them.

State how you will know each item works

  1. Acceptance evidence shows that one purchased item works, such as a form filing to the right chart. A success measure shows whether the clinic got the result it bought, such as less front-desk time per check-in.
  2. Record a success measure, such as "front-desk minutes per new-patient check-in." The clinic owns it, usually the practice administrator or another named operations leader. The clinic sets any target.
  3. Without an agreed measure, nobody captures a baseline before go-live. Then nobody can show later whether the project delivered the result.

Build a one-page client and project profile

  1. A website shows how the clinic presents itself, not how it really runs. Hours, locations, and services may be out of date. The client must confirm the project facts.
  2. Record the fax service as an outside company that may receive PHI, which may make it a business associate. Send the question to the clinic's privacy authority. That person answers it, not you.

Turn each purchased item into a named resource

  1. A data conversion lead does the work, and a client data owner approves the converted data. Other roles may be consulted, such as the practice administrator.
  2. Each role carries a different duty. If one role is missed, nobody notices when that duty is not done. Naming the roles also shows whether one person is approving their own work.

Open the control logs and file each entry correctly

  1. Risk, assumption, issue, and dependency. Ask: "Has it already happened?" If yes, it is an issue.
  2. It is an assumption. Record how and when it will be checked and who will check it, such as a device test by a set date.
  3. People forget why a choice was made. The decision log shows who decided, when, why, and what it affects. It stops the team from reopening settled questions or building against a decision nobody can find.

Worked example prompts

  1. Lab interface as to be confirmed. The client may rely on the promise, so dropping it would surprise them. Adding it would expand scope without authority. "To be confirmed" with an owner and date keeps it visible until sales and the commercial owner decide.
  2. January date as a risk. Nothing has gone wrong yet. The date may or may not be met, depending on staffing, data, interfaces, and calendars. A risk records that uncertainty and gives it a review date.
  3. Success measure before the build. The measure decides what baseline to capture before go-live. If it waits until after go-live, the "before" picture is gone and the result cannot be shown.

Faded example

A strong answer includes:

  • Step 4, scope summary. In scope: the EHR, patient portal, two consent forms, immunization-history conversion, and the registry interface. Out of scope: anything known to be excluded, such as language options if sales confirms they were not sold. To be confirmed: Spanish portal screens (sales, before kickoff); the missing proposal (sales); conversion details such as date range and who validates.
  • Step 5, acceptance and measure. Acceptance: "A parent completes the approved consent form in the portal, and it files to the child's chart." Measure: "Share of vaccine visits with a signed consent form before arrival," owned by the clinic manager. Another measurable result with a clinic owner is also correct.
  • Step 6, resources. Registry interface: integration lead (responsible) plus a clinic business owner for immunizations (accountable). The first question is who owns the registry registration, credentials, and testing. Conversion: data conversion lead (responsible) and a clinic data owner (accountable). Clinical review of the converted histories goes to the clinic's clinical leader.
  • Step 7, logs. Risk: "The registry may not approve test access before flu season." Dependency: "Interface testing depends on the registry issuing test credentials." Issue: "The proposal is referred to in the order but has not been provided."

Knowledge check

  1. The signed order sets what was sold. It is the commercial starting specification. It does not, by itself, define how each item fits the clinic's workflow, data, or duties. Discovery works that out. Unclear items, such as an undefined conversion, still need to be confirmed.
  2. Log it as a remembered commitment and list it as to be confirmed. Give it an owner (usually sales) and a due date to verify it. Plan on the documented scope until someone with authority confirms it. If it is confirmed but not covered, the commercial owner decides whether an approved change is needed.
  3. Documented: the signed order or agreement, the SOW, a proposal the order includes or attaches, and an approved change. Remembered: sales notes, emails, meeting summaries, demo conversations, and verbal promises.
  4. An assumption is something the plan treats as true but nobody has checked. You record how and when to confirm it. An issue is a problem that already exists and needs resolution now.
  5. The responsible person does the work, and the accountable person approves the result. Separating them means someone other than the doer checks the work. It also keeps decisions with the people who have authority, such as a client data owner approving converted data.
  6. Any three of: the project involves PHI; an outside company may receive patient data; a clinical workflow changes; billing or claims are affected; security access or storage is involved; a regulatory or contract duty applies.
  7. An observable result on the approved path. For example, a patient completes the approved intake form and the answers file to the correct chart, or a test claim completes the approved billing path. Configuration, licensing, or training attendance alone does not show the workflow works.
