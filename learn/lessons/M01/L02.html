Module 1, Lesson 2: Sort each commitment: documented or remembered

Sort each commitment: documented or remembered

The client may plan around a promise that was never signed. Sorting early lets the right person confirm or correct it before it turns into a dispute at go-live.

Every statement about what the client will get falls into one of two groups. Sort each one before you write the scope summary.

A documented commitment is written in a document that forms part of the sale or changes it:

  • the signed order or signed agreement;
  • the statement of work;
  • the proposal, when the signed order includes it by reference or attaches it;
  • an approved change: a change order or amendment approved by the people with authority to change the contract.

A remembered commitment is anything else. It includes sales notes, emails, meeting summaries, demo conversations, and verbal promises. It still matters, because the client may have bought the system because of it. But it is not scope until someone with authority confirms it.

Why the line falls here: many software agreements say the signed document is the whole agreement. It replaces earlier talks. You do not interpret the contract yourself; your company's contracts team does. But the line tells you where to start. The signed documents come first. Everything else is something to verify.

Two checks help with unclear cases:

  • A proposal that the order does not mention is not automatically documented. Ask sales or the contracts team whether it forms part of the sale.
  • Documented sources can conflict. If the proposal says one thing and the signed order another, log an issue now. The contracts or commercial owner decides which one controls.

Example. At a handoff for a two-location internal medicine practice, sales lists four items the client expects:

Statement Where it appears Type What you do
E-prescribing at both locations Signed order, line 4 Documented List as in scope
Twelve custom intake forms Proposal section 3, attached to the signed order Documented List as in scope; confirm the form list in discovery
Two extra training days at the second location Sales representative's email to the practice administrator Remembered Log for verification; owner is the sales representative; due before kickoff
Weekend go-live support Verbal, from the demo Remembered Log for verification; same owner and due date

The two remembered items are not in scope. They are not out of scope either. They go in as to be confirmed, with sales as owner and a due date. If sales confirms a promise the signed documents do not cover, the commercial owner decides whether an approved change is needed. Until then, you plan on the documented scope and show the open item.

Rule: A remembered commitment stays "to be confirmed," with an owner and a due date, until someone with authority confirms it.

The e-prescribing line and the weekend-support promise look alike to the client. Both are things they expect. The source is what separates them: one is signed, one must be verified.

Check yourself

  1. Name the four sources that make a commitment documented.
  2. A proposal mentions a patient check-in kiosk, but the signed order does not mention the proposal. How do you treat the kiosk, and why?
  3. Why do you not list a remembered promise as out of scope?

Write the initial scope summary

The team plans from the scope summary. If uncertainty hides inside broad words, the team plans work that nobody has defined.

After the handoff, write a short scope summary. It is not the final project plan. It is the clearest current statement of the work the sale created. Use three parts.

In scope. List the documented systems, modules, locations, user groups, conversion items, interfaces, devices, configuration, training, and go-live support.

Out of scope. List known exclusions. This protects both sides. Examples: redesigning the whole billing department, fixing old source data, supplying unsupported hardware, or building an interface that was discussed but not bought.

To be confirmed. List items that need discovery or approval, each with an owner and due date. Do not hide uncertainty inside broad scope words.

Rule: List anything not yet defined as "to be confirmed," never inside broad scope words.

Example A. The order says "e-prescribing at both locations." The item, the places, and the users are clear. It goes in scope.

Example B. The order says "data conversion." It does not name the sources, record counts, date range, or who checks the results. It goes in scope as purchased, with each open question listed as to be confirmed.

Both items are on the signed order. The difference is whether the work is defined well enough to plan.

Check yourself

  1. Name the three parts of the initial scope summary.
  2. The order says "data conversion" and nothing more. How do you record it, and why?

State how you will know each item works

If nobody says what "working" looks like, the team will call configured work finished. If nobody agrees how to measure the clinic's goal, nobody can show later that the project delivered it.

Acceptance evidence is the proof that a purchased item works. Write a simple statement for each major item now:

  • authorized users can schedule, reschedule, and cancel an appointment using the approved workflow;
  • a patient can complete the approved electronic intake form, and the answers file to the correct chart;
  • a test claim completes the approved billing path;
  • sample converted records match the approved source and mapping rules.

These statements will get more specific during discovery and testing. Starting them now keeps the project from using "configured" in place of "works."

A success measure shows whether the clinic got the result it bought. For each purchased outcome, agree how success will be measured and who owns the measure. Suppose the clinic bought the system to stop entering intake twice. A measure could be "front-desk minutes per new-patient check-in," owned by the practice administrator. The clinic sets any target. The starting value, called the baseline, is captured before go-live (Module 11). The results are compared after go-live (Module 13).

Rule: Write each acceptance statement and success measure as a result someone can observe.

Example A. "Intake forms are configured" describes setup. "A patient completes the approved form online, and the answers file to the right chart" describes a result.

Example B. "Better billing" is a hope. "Share of claims accepted on first submission, owned by the billing manager" is a measure.

In both, the second version names something a person can watch happen or count.

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between acceptance evidence and a success measure?
  2. The sponsor says the new system should "save the front desk time." What do you record, and who owns it?
  3. Why agree on the measure now, months before go-live?

Build a one-page client and project profile

The team needs the same basic facts to plan. The profile also brings privacy and security questions to the right people early, before they block the build.

Research only what helps the team plan. A useful profile includes:

  • organization type and services;
  • locations and operating hours;
  • approximate user groups and departments;
  • project sponsor and day-to-day owner;
  • current system and reason for change;
  • purchased system, modules, and services;
  • intended go-live window;
  • known outside companies and interfaces;
  • known regulatory, contract, security, or record-keeping duties;
  • major clinic events that affect the calendar.

A website gives context, but it is not the source for the clinic's internal operations. The client confirms the project facts. If the clinic did a readiness assessment before buying, attach its results. Readiness is checked again before go-live (Module 11).

Find out early whether the project involves protected health information (PHI). PHI is health information that identifies a patient. Find out which outside companies may act as business associates, meaning vendors that handle PHI for the clinic. Find out who owns the clinic's privacy and security review. Your first job is to place those questions with that authority, not to answer them yourself.

Rule: Record each privacy or security question and send it to the client's named authority; do not answer it yourself.

Example A. The order includes an appointment-reminder service that will receive patient names and phone numbers. You list it as an outside company that may receive PHI. You send the question to the privacy officer.

Example B. The clinic plans to keep exported data in an online storage service it already uses. You note it and send the question to the clinic's security owner.

In both cases, you spot the question and route it. The clinic's authority answers it.

Check yourself

  1. Why is the clinic's website not enough as a source for the profile?
  2. An outside fax service will receive referral documents. What do you record, and who answers the question?
